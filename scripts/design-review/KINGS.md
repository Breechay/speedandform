# Audited against the people who solved this already

Not inspiration. Each of these is an instrument that had the same problem this
surface has — a lot of facts, most of them routine, one of them decisive — and
each solved one element of it. What we take, and where we are not allowed to stop
where they stopped.

---

## Jan Tschichold · the measure

**The element:** the page as a bounded field. Tschichold's Penguin Composition
Rules exist because a text block that runs to the paper's edge stops being a
page and becomes a surface with type on it.

**Taken:** 1180px, centred, at every viewport. The week is a page.

**Where we go further:** a book's margin is empty by convention. Ours has a job —
the right margin is where the rung hangs, aligned to the same axis as
`CAN ESTABLISH`. The margin carries the one thing the week is trying to prove.

## Massimo Vignelli · one grid, few sizes

**The element:** the 1972 NYC subway diagram and the Knoll system. A small number
of type sizes, ruthlessly reused, so that a size *means* something.

**Taken:** four registers and no more — identity (Newsreader 44/36), figures
(56/27/21/19/15), body (12.5), labels (8.5 caps, .18em). Any new element must be
one of the four.

**Where we go further:** Vignelli's system is uniform by principle. Ours is
deliberately non-uniform where the content is — a 128px row beside a 75px row —
because a week is not a uniform object and drawing it as one is a lie about the
training. Uniformity in the *vocabulary*, variation in the *rhythm*.

## Edward Tufte · data-ink

**The element:** every drop of ink should carry information. Boxes that only
separate are ink that says nothing.

**Taken:** the row fills are gone. Seven filled cards became seven positions on a
hairline. The only fill left on the surface is today, and it is there because it
carries a fact.

**Where we go further:** Tufte's instinct is to remove until nothing decorative
remains, which on a dark instrument produces something that reads as unfinished.
We keep exactly one non-informational element — a single soft top-light behind
the hero, 70s drift, 4px. Atmosphere is not data, and it never earns its
existence with a number.

## Hans Hilfiker · the single red hand

**The element:** the Swiss railway clock. One red element on a black-and-white
face; everything else is timekeeping.

**Taken:** lime is one word. This is why V2 takes lime off `today` — production
spends the accent on the day the athlete is standing in, which they already know,
and then has nothing left to say *this can establish something*. Today became
light. Lime became the claim.

**Where we go further:** Hilfiker's red hand marks the present. Ours marks the
future — the thing not yet proved. That is the harder and more useful signal.

## Otl Aicher · the margin does the organising

**The element:** Munich '72 and the ERCO manuals. Structure carried by the
hanging edge rather than by ruled boxes.

**Taken:** day names hang right-aligned in a 96px column against a 1px spine that
runs the whole week unbroken. No rule under every row — that is a spreadsheet,
and space does the separating.

**Where we go further:** Aicher's grid is inert. Our spine is stateful — it reads
`#ffffff12` for a day that carries the athlete, `#ffffff30` for a day that bears
on the mark, lime for a day that can move it. The organising line is also the
information.

## Bloomberg Terminal · earned density

**The element:** the argument for density. A terminal is legitimately dense
because every glyph is a fact a trader is paid to read, and the alignment is
strict enough to scan.

**Taken:** tabular numerals everywhere figures stack, so `9 MI · 6 MI · 8 MI`
align on the same axis and the week's volume can be read down a column.

**Where we reject it:** a terminal is a surface for someone paid to be there. An
athlete opens this on a phone at 6am. The five accounting facts get one line each
on the phone precisely so the *work* clears the fold. Density is earned per
reader, not per screen.

## Dieter Rams · the instrument face

**The element:** the ET66 and the RT20. A neutral field, one bright element,
labels smaller than values, nothing that pretends to be more important than the
thing being measured.

**Taken:** labels at 8.5px against figures at 21–56. The controls (nav, lens,
Photo Lab, week arrows) are boxed hairlines that fill on press — no capsules
anywhere. A rounded pill on this surface reads as a different application.

**Where we go further:** Rams's objects have no editorial voice. This one leads
with a sentence in a serif — *How far can you hold 6:30–6:45 without it coming
apart?* — because the product's claim is that an athlete has a question, not a
number. The instrument is subordinate to the question, which is the reverse of
every training dashboard.

## The one we are not better than yet

**The newspaper front page.** A broadsheet handles exactly our unsolved problem:
a page of items of wildly different weight, where the reader takes in the
hierarchy before reading a single word. It does it with column structure, not
with size alone — the lead has a *shape*, not just a bigger headline.

Our week still reads as one column of rows in which one row is taller. Five easy
days in a run is a single fact — *35 easy miles across five days* — and we draw
it as five statements. Closing that is the next real move on this surface, and it
needs markup rather than CSS.
