Reference / Sleep and recovery

Sleep is part
of the workout.

The run or lift gives your body an instruction. Sleep gives it time to respond. You need the session, the food and the nights that follow.

What your night is doing

Three shifts.
One night.

Sleep moves between light, deep and REM stages several times. Each has a job. None works alone.

Pattern only, not a measured night or a target for stage percentages. A watch estimates these stages; a sleep study measures them.

N1 + N2Light

The doorway and the quiet hallway.

N1 is how you drift off. N2 helps you stay asleep while the world keeps moving. Your heart rate and breathing slow. Brief bursts of brain activity called sleep spindles are linked with learning.

Light sleep is real sleep. It usually takes the biggest share of the night.

N3Deep

The maintenance shift.

Breathing and heart rate reach lower levels, and waking you is harder. Deep sleep is associated with a strong early pulse of growth hormone. Sleep as a whole supports repair and immune function.

Deep minutes are not a muscle-growth counter.

REMDream

The rehearsal room.

Your brain is active, dreams often become vivid, and most muscles are temporarily kept still. REM plays a part in memory and emotional processing. The last portion of the night often contains longer REM periods.

Skills and memories also depend on non-REM sleep. There is no single magic stage.

Sleep-stage description: NIH and NINDS.

The point

You cannot out-train a missing night.

Think of training like planting. Running and lifting put something in the ground. Food, recovery and enough sleep help it grow. Digging more holes does not make the plants grow faster.

Most adults need roughly seven to nine hours of sleep. That is a starting point, not a stopwatch target; an athlete in heavy training may need more opportunity. One poor night does not erase your fitness. Repeated short or broken nights can make the work harder to absorb.

01 / Running

The pace is the same.
The cost changes.

Sleep loss can make concentration, judgment and endurance work harder. You might know your pace but struggle to settle into it. That does not automatically mean you lost fitness overnight.

Sleep belongs in the training week, just like the easy day after a hard session. Before moving a workout earlier, ask whether the alarm steals the recovery that workout needs.

Try: protect the night before a demanding run. If an early start makes enough sleep impossible, discuss moving the session rather than treating less sleep as the price of discipline.

02 / Lifting and size

The workout is the order.
Recovery fills it.

Lifting tells muscle to adapt. Food provides building material. Sleep helps create the conditions for that work. In small controlled studies, severe sleep restriction reduced measured muscle protein synthesis. That does not tell us how much muscle any one athlete gains from an extra hour.

What about growth hormone and testosterone?

Growth hormone often has a large pulse around early deep sleep. Testosterone has a relationship with sleep and the body clock, but short-sleep experiments have produced mixed results. Neither hormone can be read from a watch's REM or deep-sleep score.

There is no bedtime trick that replaces progressive training, enough food and weeks of recovery.

Try: plan your sleep opportunity when you plan a hard lift. Do not turn a late gym session plus an early alarm into your normal routine.

03 / People

The person you love
gets your recovery, too.

When you're short on sleep, a normal question can feel like criticism. Patience and empathy may drop before you notice. Research on sleep loss and couples suggests that poor sleep can make conflict harder to handle.

This is not an excuse to be unkind. It is a reason to notice the state you're bringing into a conversation.

Try: “I'm running low. Give me ten minutes to reset, and I want to hear you.” Then come back. Protecting sleep can also mean agreeing on a quiet window together.

A seven-night field test

Find the leak.
Change one thing.

Not all bad sleep has the same cause. Pick the situation that looks like yours. Run one experiment for seven nights, then judge how you feel and train. Do not try six fixes at once.

AThe early workout steals sleep

Make the morning earn its place. Start with when you must wake. Work backward to leave a realistic sleep window and time to fall asleep. If the math does not work, move an easy session or change the start time when possible.

Notice: morning alertness and how the usual easy pace feels.

BYour body is tired, but your head is still racing

Make a landing strip. After work or late training, give yourself a short, repeatable transition: wash up, put tomorrow's first task on paper, lower the light. You're not trying to force sleep. You're giving your brain permission to stop working.

Notice: time spent awake before falling asleep.

CThe afternoon caffeine is doing double duty

Move the caffeine clock. Try finishing caffeine at least eight hours before your intended bedtime for a week. Large doses can disrupt sleep even when taken many hours earlier. Do not change your training and caffeine pattern on the same day if you're trying to learn which matters.

Notice: sleep onset, waking during the night and next-day energy.

DYour household runs on different clocks

Design a quiet handoff. Agree on a protected window. Set out early-run gear outside the bedroom. Move the bright-light, alarm and coffee routine away from the sleeping person. Keep a separate ten-minute connection window so sleep isn't framed as choosing training over someone else.

Notice: interruptions and whether the plan works for both people.

EYou wake up and start fighting the clock

Stop turning the bed into a contest. Keep clocks out of view. If you're awake, frustrated and no longer sleepy, leave the bed for a calm, dimly lit activity. Return when sleepy. Persistent insomnia deserves proper care; sleep tips alone are not treatment.

Notice: how much effort you spend trying to force sleep.

FYour sleep score has become another workout

Hide the stage percentages for a week. A watch is estimating, not reading your brain waves. Log only the time you gave yourself to sleep, how rested you feel and how your normal session went. Look for a pattern, not a verdict from one night.

Notice: less worry and a clearer link between sleep and the day.

Give the test a fair chance.

Use the same question each morning: “Did I give myself enough chance to sleep, and how do I feel?” Add one note after training: “Did the usual work feel normal or unusually expensive?”

If the experiment helps, keep it. If it does not, change the hypothesis. Your life, schedule and body matter more than a perfect graph.

When a tip isn't enough

Get help for a problem that keeps returning.

Loud habitual snoring, gasping during sleep, severe daytime sleepiness or insomnia that keeps affecting your life deserves a conversation with a clinician. Cognitive behavioral therapy for insomnia (CBT-I) is a first-line treatment for chronic insomnia; ordinary sleep-hygiene tips alone are not.

Sleep tracking is useful for patterns, not for diagnosing a disorder or grading a person's recovery from one REM number.
