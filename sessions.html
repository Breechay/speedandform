What is each run for?

Different runs practice different jobs: repeatable endurance, sustained stronger effort or quicker movement. Choose the job, then read the effort, amount of work and recovery.

Use the right tool.

Before a session, ask: What am I practicing today? The answer gives you a way to judge the run beyond whether you felt tired afterward. [1]

Easy runs: practice you can repeat.

Run at a conversational effort. Build endurance without making every day demanding. [2]

Example: 30 easy minutes, if that duration is already comfortable for you. Walking breaks can keep the effort manageable.

Success looks like: you finish with room left and can keep the week going. Learn easy running →

Threshold: hold a strong effort.

Strong, sustained running with control. It develops your ability to keep a demanding effort going.

Example: three six-minute pieces, with two minutes easy jogging between them, for a runner ready for faster work.

Success looks like: you hold the intended effort and recover well. See the complete example →

Long runs: stay out longer.

Usually easy in pace, longer in time. Practice patience, endurance, and the food and drink you will need.

Example: a familiar 60-minute easy run. If your recent longest run is 30 minutes, this is not your next automatic step.

Success looks like: you manage the duration and recover for the rest of the week. Choose the long run →

Intervals: work, recover, repeat.

“Interval” describes a format: pieces of running with breaks between them. The pieces might be threshold work, faster aerobic work, hills, or another purpose. It is not one effort zone.

Longer fast repetitions can be used to challenge your ability to use oxygen at a high rate. Shorter work with more rest can focus on speed and coordination. Pace, duration and recovery change the task. [3]

Ask for all three. A list of distances without effort or recovery is an incomplete instruction.

Strides: a brief taste of quicker running.

A stride is a short, smooth acceleration. Build speed gradually, stay relaxed, then ease down. It is not an all-out sprint.

Keep it brief and recover enough to repeat smoothly. The exact number and length depend on your plan and readiness. They still count as training; short does not mean cost-free.

Success looks like: quick, organized running without straining to win the last repetition.

Recovery: help the next run happen.

A recovery run is deliberately gentle and manageable. Sometimes walking or rest is the better choice. You do not have to run to earn recovery.

An easy hour is not automatically recovery just because the pace was slow. Duration still counts. Run easy, reduce or rest? →

Can you explain the session before you start?

  1. 01

    Name the job

    For example: hold a strong effort with control.

  2. 02

    Read the full instruction

    Warm-up, work, effort, recovery, and cool-down.

  3. 03

    Know when to change it

    What do you do if the effort or movement stops matching the job?

These examples illustrate the types of running. They are not a week to combine. Choose the mix from your current ability, goal and recovery.

Use it this week.

Before 1 planned session this week, write 4 things: its job, the effort, the amount of work and the recovery. If any are missing, clarify the instruction before starting.

Three mistakes to catch.

  1. Assuming “intervals” means one particular speed.
  2. Judging the session only by exhaustion.
  3. Combining every example on this page into one week.

Where does it fit?

Session types are the pieces of a training week. Learn the pieces here, then use the week lesson to arrange them.

The part to take with you

Remember this.

  1. A workout name does not tell you the whole job.
  2. Effort, work time and recovery belong together.
  3. More exhausted does not mean more successful.
Your plan says “intervals.” Do you know how fast to run yet?

No. Interval means work separated by recovery. You still need the purpose, effort, amount of work and recovery instructions. Threshold sessions can use intervals too.
