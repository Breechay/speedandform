Give the run enough fuel.

Eat regularly, arrive ready for the session, and practice food and drink for longer runs. The plan should fit the duration, effort, and what your stomach tolerates.

By Brice IkouebeUpdated September 17, 2026

In this guide

Before: keep it familiar.

For a longer or harder session, leave time for a familiar meal or have a smaller carbohydrate-rich snack when you are running early. Toast, a banana, or a small bowl of oats are options, not a compulsory menu. Meal size and timing should fit your tolerance. [1]

Do not force a large breakfast just before leaving. Equally, fasted running is not an entry requirement for easy training. When going out hungry repeatedly leaves you flat, change the routine. A short run needing no food during it does not mean the day needs less food.

During: duration and effort change the job.

These are general carbohydrate ranges, not an instruction to jump straight to the highest number. Count the whole session, including its warm-up, recoveries, and cool-down. [2]

Short, easy run

Usually no mid-run foodRegular meals and arriving adequately fueled are usually enough. Bring fluid as conditions require.

About 1–2.5 hours

30–60 g per hourA common working range for sustained endurance exercise. Choose a tolerable starting amount and practice it.

Longer than about 2.5 hours

Up to 90 g per hourAn option for prolonged demanding exercise with practiced tolerance, often using glucose plus fructose. Not a requirement for every runner.

Hard efforts around an hour can also benefit from small amounts of carbohydrate. There is no magic cutoff where food suddenly becomes useful. [2]

Turn the label into a plan.

Check your amounts.

Arithmetic, not a recommendation. Enter the carbohydrates you plan to consume, not just carry. Read each product’s label.

Calculate to see the total and hourly average.

Check gels, chews, food, and drinks together. Two different products are not two separate fueling plans. Carry a little contingency for a delayed finish, but do not mistake the amount packed for the amount eaten.

Fluids are a separate decision.

Plan access to drink, especially in heat. Your fluid needs depend on conditions, sweat losses, pace, and time outside. Do not drink extra liters just to reach a carbohydrate target; use food or gels alongside an appropriate fluid plan.

Both too little and too much fluid can be harmful. Avoid forced drinking beyond your needs. Electrolytes do not make overdrinking safe, and a universal bottle-per-hour rule is not an individual hydration plan. [3]

After: eat, then keep eating normally.

A meal or snack with carbohydrate and protein is a practical next step: rice with beans and eggs, yogurt with oats and fruit, or a sandwich and a drink. Eat enough over the day, not just in a narrow post-run window. When another demanding session is close, plan recovery eating sooner. [1]

Persistent fatigue, repeated injuries, or menstrual changes deserve professional attention. Low energy availability is one possible contributor, not a diagnosis from a checklist. A sports dietitian and clinician can help assess the pattern. [4]

Practice before you race.

My stomach struggles with gels.

Try familiar alternatives, smaller divided amounts, and the water recommended with the product. Check concentration and what you ate beforehand. Repeated fueling practice improved gastrointestinal tolerance in a small study of endurance runners, but the study’s high intake is not a starting target for everyone. [5]

Do not train through persistent vomiting, significant abdominal pain, or inability to keep fluids down. Seek medical advice for recurring or severe symptoms.

Should I try a higher intake on race day?

No new fueling experiment on race day. Use an amount, timing, and products you have practiced under similar conditions. A high number on someone else’s plan is not evidence that it suits you.

Do I need supplements or caffeine?

You can build a useful fueling routine from ordinary food and familiar sports products. Caffeine is optional, not a missing requirement. Do not add an unfamiliar product just because the session matters.

Sources & context

The examples and decisions above are FORM coaching guidance. The research below supports the stated principles, not an individual prescription or a guaranteed result.

  1. Thomas, Erdman and Burke, 2016Nutrition and Athletic Performance: joint position statementPublished guidance from the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, Dietitians of Canada and ACSM. Food type, amount, timing and individual needs all matter; examples here are not a personalized diet.
  2. Jeukendrup, 2014Carbohydrate intake during exerciseAn author review drawing mainly on trained endurance athletes. Duration, intensity, carbohydrate type and tolerance matter; these common ranges are not universal minima or physiological ceilings.
  3. National Athletic Trainers’ Association, 2017Fluid replacement for the physically activeThe association’s explanation of its position statement addresses underdrinking and overdrinking. Fluid replacement is individual, including when sports drinks are used.
  4. Mountjoy et al., 2023IOC consensus on Relative Energy Deficiency in SportProblematic low energy availability affects women and men. Symptoms overlap other conditions and require appropriate clinical assessment.
  5. Miall et al., 2018Repeated fueling practice and gastrointestinal toleranceA small randomized gut-training study in endurance runners. It supports practicing tolerance, not starting every runner at the study’s high carbohydrate intake.
