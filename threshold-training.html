The running library · 02 / Threshold training

Run strong. Keep it controlled.

Threshold work is sustained, demanding running that stays controlled. The aim is useful time at a strong effort, not the fastest possible final repetition.

By Brice IkouebeUpdated September 17, 2026

What the effort should feel like.

Breathing is deeper than on an easy run. Short phrases are possible, but conversation is no longer relaxed. You are concentrating on the work without fighting to complete each minute. Those are coaching cues, not a blood-lactate test.

Easy running

You can talk comfortably.

Threshold work

Strong, focused, controlled.

Too hard for this job

You are racing the last repetition.

Some increase in effort as a session progresses is normal. Repeated slowing, straining, or needing much longer rests is a reason to adjust. Do not turn “controlled” into a requirement that every repetition feels identical.

What “threshold” means here.

Coaches use the word threshold in different ways. Here it means controlled sustained work near the upper end of your sustainable aerobic effort. Physiological threshold methods do not all identify the same speed, and the first rise in blood lactate is not the same boundary as the highest sustainable steady state. [1]

Lactate is not waste you need to flush out. It is also a fuel used during exercise. Fatigue cannot be reduced to a single substance or a line on a watch. [2]

The practical coaching goal is to develop sustained running with a dose you can recover from. Threshold is part of the training, not a guarantee of improvement at every race distance.

Start with a session you can control.

Keep the first repetition restrained. Use a recent, established training pace as a starting point, then adjust for effort and conditions. Do not calculate threshold from a race time you hope to run.

For someone new to running or returning after a break, consistent easy running comes first.

Progress the work, not the struggle.

The repetitions stay controlled and recovery is good.
Repeat the session. Later, extend the work a little before asking for more speed. Change one demand at a time.
You start too fast.
Slow the next repetition. Finishing the planned volume is less important than recovering the intended effort.
You cannot recover between repetitions.
Reduce the remaining work or finish easily. Longer rests change the session; they do not make an unsustainable pace the right target.
Heat, hills, or fatigue change the pace.
Keep the purpose. Adjust pace or duration instead of forcing cool-weather numbers. [3]

One threshold day is a useful starting structure in my coaching, supported by easier work around it. More experienced athletes can use other arrangements. Tuesday is a scheduling choice, not a physiological rule.

Threshold, tempo, and race pace.

Is a tempo run the same thing?

Not always. “Tempo” is used for everything from moderately steady running to threshold work. Check the actual effort, duration, and recovery rather than treating the labels as interchangeable.

Is threshold my half-marathon pace?

Not automatically. Race pace depends on the race distance and how long you take to cover it. A faster runner and a slower runner can race the same distance at different intensities relative to their thresholds. Establish your own pace instead of applying one fixed gap to everyone.

Should I finish as fast as I can?

No. Finish the prescribed work. A sprint at the end answers a different question from the one this session asks. Save the race effort for an appropriate session or the race itself.
