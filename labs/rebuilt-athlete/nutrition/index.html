The Rebuilt Athlete / Fuel & appearance

Fuel the
rebuild.

Eat to run, lift, heal, and look like yourself. A simple working food system built around what Brice enjoys and how the body responds.

Current phase / Bridge Season

Muscular near 185 lb. Running durability, strength, bone health, hair and skin. Enjoyable food and enough energy are part of the work. This is a reference, not a restrictive diet.

01

The foods doing the work.

The combination feels light, sturdy, and satisfying. Each food has a different job. There is no need to replace this foundation.

01 / Fuel + fiber

Oats + flax

Oats provide carbohydrate for running. Ground flax adds essential ALA omega-3 fats, fiber, lignans, and an easy way to raise energy intake without a bigger bowl.

Try 1–2 tbsp ground flax. Two tbsp add about 75 kcal. Not a proven hair-regrowth treatment.
02 / Ready energy

Jasmine rice

Easily digested carbohydrate helps refill glycogen after long runs and supports the next session. Around 45 g carbohydrate per cooked cup.

Keep as a reliable base. Pair with protein and colorful foods.
03 / Building material

Egg whites

Six large whites bring about 22 g complete protein for muscle repair. Very lean and easy to combine with rice.

They contain little iron, vitamin D, vitamin A or choline. Cover those elsewhere.
04 / Recovery drink

Chocolate milk

Carbohydrate, complete protein, fluid and calcium in one convenient drink. Especially useful after long runs or when appetite is low.

Check the label for vitamin D. Helpful fuel, not a full-day meal replacement.
02

Eat around the work.

No rigid eating clock. On a training morning, give your legs easy carbohydrate first. Bring protein, fiber and more fat into the meals afterward. Build the day from small things you enjoy.

Before · 30 minutes to 3 hours

Make running feel easy to fuel.

With a couple of hours to digest, try jasmine rice or a modest oatmeal bowl with maple syrup. If a very early workout leaves only 15–30 minutes, a small, familiar carbohydrate bite may be enough. Keep most flax, spinach, steak and whole milk for later if fiber or fat feels heavy before running.

Your pairing: rice or simple oats + maple syrup, with water.
After · First hour or so

Begin the refill.

Chocolate milk is a convenient first step after running, especially longer sessions or speedwork. It supplies carbohydrate, protein and fluid. Think of it as a bridge to food, not the entire recovery meal. There is no stopwatch deadline if a normal meal is close.

Your pairing: a glass of chocolate milk, then a meal.
Next meal · Within a couple of hours

Carbs meet protein.

Jasmine rice refills muscle fuel. Six to eight egg whites contribute roughly 22–29 g protein. Spinach adds micronutrients, and kiwi brings vitamin C. Use more rice after a long or hard run than after a short easy one, according to appetite and the next session.

Your pairing: rice + egg whites + spinach; kiwi after.
Later · Lunch, afternoon or evening

Complete the day.

Make the satisfying oats-and-flax bowl here. Whole milk adds protein, calcium and fat; maple syrup adds carbohydrate. Cottage cheese or turkey can fill a protein gap between meals. A steak meal once a week can replace the egg whites at lunch or dinner.

Your pairing: oats + whole milk + ground flax, with a separate protein food as needed.
The pairing rule: Put carbohydrate close to running; pair carbohydrate and protein afterward; spread protein across the day. Aim for roughly 25–40 g protein at several eating occasions rather than one enormous meal. Your full-day intake matters more than minute-perfect timing.

Long runs · Around 75–90 min or more

Start fed and practice portable carbohydrate during the run, working toward about 30–60 g per hour as tolerated. Take fluids according to conditions and sweat loss; Miami heat may increase fluid and electrolyte needs. Recover with chocolate milk, then rice and protein.

Speed + strength days

Don't arrive depleted. Use digestible carbs before hard running and a protein-containing meal afterward. On strength-only days, eat sufficient protein across meals. Oats and flax fit well later once training is done.

Yoga, walking + easier days

Keep protein, calcium and enough total food even when the running load is light. Enjoy oats, flax, milk, cottage cheese and rice in the amounts that feel right. Don't turn an easy day into an aggressive fast, especially after a long run.

An example 6 a.m. running morning

  1. Before · 5:15–5:40Small rice portion or another familiar carbohydrate if needed; water.
  2. After · 7:00–7:30Chocolate milk on the way home.
  3. Breakfast · 7:30–9:00Rice, egg whites, spinach and kiwi.
  4. Later · FlexibleOats with whole milk, flax and maple; cottage cheese, turkey or steak at another meal.

These are examples, not rules or a calorie prescription. For short easy sessions, a pre-run snack may be unnecessary if you feel well. For long or hard sessions, test carbohydrate amounts during training rather than trying something new on an important day.

03

Protect the whole athlete.

Muscle and bone repair continue on easy days. A limited menu can be comfortable yet still miss important nutrients. Small additions are enough to increase variety.

Calcium + vitamin D

Bone structure and calcium absorption. Milk and cottage cheese help with calcium; check for added D on milk labels. General adult targets: 1,000 mg calcium and 600 IU vitamin D daily.

Iron + zinc

Iron carries oxygen. Zinc supports normal tissue repair and hair health. Lean beef can contribute both, along with B12.

Vitamin C

Helps make collagen and improves absorption of plant iron. Kiwi is an easy, low-volume addition.

Lutein + carotenoids

Spinach brings nutrients important for eye health. Add it to egg whites or alongside rice.

Enough dietary fat

Flax is useful but may not cover all fat needs. Olive oil can add energy without nuts or avocado.

Enough total food

At about 185 lb, 120–170 g protein daily is a broad training range. Adequate calories matter for muscle, bone and hair, not just workout performance.

HAIR + SKIN

Support growth. Avoid promises.

Low energy intake or shortages of protein, iron or zinc can contribute to hair shedding. Food cannot reliably reverse hereditary crown thinning. For progressive thinning, a dermatologist can distinguish causes and decide whether labs are useful. For skin, keep sleep, normal hydration and sun protection consistent.

UNDER-EYE APPEARANCE

Find the actual cause.

Puffiness, shadows and hollows are not the same thing. Sleep, allergies, fluid retention and inherited anatomy can each play a role. A cool compress may temporarily reduce puffiness; no single food removes structural bags. Monitor the pattern before buying treatments.

04

Simple decisions.

ONCE A WEEK IS REASONABLE

Make room for steak.

One lean, unprocessed steak meal weekly is a practical option, not a requirement. It brings complete protein, more readily absorbed heme iron, zinc and B12. Choose a lean cut such as sirloin or top round, and combine it with rice and spinach. Keep portions moderate, around 3–5 oz cooked, and let poultry, dairy and other foods share the week.

HIGHEST VALUE FIRST

Food before a supplement stack.

Keep your existing protein powder for days you miss protein. Creatine monohydrate, 3–5 g daily, is a well-studied optional supplement for strength and repeated hard efforts, but it does not treat hair thinning or replace fueling. It may raise scale weight through muscle water. Vitamin D is worth discussing if intake or blood levels are low, especially with the fracture history. Do not start iron or zinc on speculation.

What is
fortified milk?

Fortified means nutrients were added to a food. Cow's milk naturally provides calcium and protein; many U.S. milks have vitamin D added, and some also have vitamin A added. Chocolate milk can be fortified too. Look for “Vitamin D” on the Nutrition Facts label, often about 2.5 mcg (100 IU) per cup, but the amount varies. Added calcium in fortified plant milks is different from milk's naturally occurring calcium. There is no special brand you have to buy.

Nutrition observations here are based on Brice's reported food response, not proof of causation. A clinician can assess persistent hair loss or bone-health concerns. Creatine is best reviewed with a clinician if kidney disease or relevant medical conditions are present.
