Mythology Inside Reality

The thesis. This is what the eye is actually looking for.

Every image you've ever saved contains an ordinary thing
elevated into something archetypal.
Not fantasy. Not cinematic. Archetype.
What the eye is actually reading
Runner on wet trackNot Simon. Persistence. Repetition. Form.
Monk on bridgeNot Japan. Ritual. Movement with intention. Presence.
Egyptian templeNot architecture. A portal. Human inside ancient structure.
Rio beach footballNot sport. A celebration of aliveness.
Espresso machineNot equipment. Ritual.
Vinyl selectionNot music. Curation as ceremony.
Audi in fogNot a car. A machine emerging from darkness.
Jungle houseNot a building. A sanctuary.
The visual hierarchy — in order
1.
Geometry

Architecture. Cards. Temples. Bridges. Track lanes. The eye finds structure first. Always. Lines, frames, symmetry, repetition — before subject.

2.
Atmosphere

Fog. Rain. Steam. Dust. Light. Atmosphere softens geometry. Weather is production value — it was already there.

3.
Human Presence

Not portraits. Presence. Someone inhabiting the space. Small humans inside large systems. The individual inside structure.

4.
Narrative

Very subtle. Never obvious. Questions over answers. The image implies a world — it does not explain one.

Shooting doctrine — derived from the references
Shoot Architecture First
Even when shooting people. Find lines, frames, symmetry, repetition before the subject arrives. The structure is the frame for the mythology.
Shoot Weather
Rain. Steam. Fog. Morning light. Golden hour. Weather is free production design. It appears in nearly every reference image. Wait for it.
Shoot Small / Large
Temple. Bridge. Track. A small human inside a large structure is never just a photo. Scale creates meaning.
Shoot Ritual
Not action. Not events. Ritual. Lacing shoes. Pouring coffee. Selecting vinyl. The ceremony inside the ordinary.
The color grade is the last 5%.
The capture is doing the work.
Your taste is deeper than a LUT.
What other photographers shoot

Events. Subjects. Aesthetics. "Look what happened." "Look who is here."

What FORM shoots

Meaning. "What does this represent?" The references describe a worldview, not a color palette.

Studio, not site
FORM is the front door. Studio is the back room. Training pages stay compressed and athlete-facing. The perception manual holds the authored worldview — how things are seen, selected, shot, edited, and remembered. It connects FORM, Hideout, cinema, and personal practice without turning them into a public biography.

Things Shaped by Time

The aesthetic foundation

FORM is attracted to beauty that emerges from cultivation,
restraint, and time. Not luxury. Not perfection. Cultivation.

A body trained for years. Moss growing across stone. A weathered building in afternoon light. A carefully composed room. A bonsai shaped through restraint. Beauty is rarely created in the moment it is photographed. It is usually the visible result of a long process already underway.

FORM documents evidence of this process.
Not performances for the camera.

Six Governing Principles

These are the doctrine. LUTs and presets are implementation.

I.
Materiality +

Light reveals texture. The grade protects it. Never manufacture it.

Everything in the FORM world is tactile: wet skin, rain jackets, terrarium moss, ocean water, condensation, freckles, puddles, glass reflections, sweat, bark, stone, aged plaster. Clarity near zero. Dehaze near zero. When grading: ask what surface is being illuminated.

II.
Luminosity +

The shadows glow. The blacks breathe. The image feels alive from within.

Luminosity over contrast. Preserve the feeling that light exists inside the scene — not merely striking its surfaces.

III.
Movement +

The athlete is not posing. He is doing. The room is not staged. It has been lived in.

In FIELD: running, diving, ocean, rain, water in motion. In STILL: the quality of arrested time — a space that feels like someone just left.

IV.
Attention +

The camera records what the eye learned to notice. The photographer discovers beauty. The camera does not create it.

FORM images feel like someone was paying attention. Shoot what you actually see — not what you think looks good.

V.
Restraint +

Restraint selects. Minimalism removes. They are not the same thing.

They're rich. But they're organized. One thing speaks at a time. Masculine without aggression. Beautiful without decoration. Soft without weakness. This is the FORM register.

VI.
Revealed Atmosphere +

FORM does not manufacture atmosphere. FORM reveals it.

Mood is not the enemy. Manufactured mood is. The universal test: if the grade becomes more interesting than the experience itself, pull it back. The goal is not cinematic. The goal is unusually alive.

The image should make you want to

"Inhabit the moment."

Not

"Admire the edit."

Selection

The first and most important step.

The biggest skill isn't grading. It's choosing.

You don't make the image in camera. You make it here — in the moment of recognition before the shutter fires, and in the brutal editing afterward.

01
Attention
Notice something worth noticing
02
Selection
Choose what matters
03
Capture
Record it with intention
04
Grade
Support what's real
Choose images that contain
Honest moments over performative ones
Context over isolation — subject in its world
Imperfect timing over forced timing
Environmental portraits over headshots
Scenes with depth, reflections, and textures
Weathered beauty — imperfection that reads as premium
Images where the air itself is visible
Delete immediately
—
Posing without purpose
—
Clean perfection — not honest
—
Orange skin — never
—
Empty backgrounds — context is always the point
—
Over-composition — let the moment compose itself
—
Anything you kept because you worked hard for it
—
Any image that looks like other people's images
Luxury through selection, not luxury through perfection.

Why did you stop and save that puddle? That runner? That terrarium? The answer isn't exposure settings. It's because you recognized something worth looking at. Selection is that recognition — trained, sharpened, then trusted.

Shooting Contexts

Six worlds. What to point the camera at when living normal life.

You are not a travel photographer.
You are not a fashion photographer.
You are Brice — which means 90% of your opportunities
are already in front of you every day.
The FORM Shot List — six categories, four arcs each
I — Self
Ritual · Transition · Solitude · Recovery
Tying shoes. Opening blinds. Making coffee. Journaling. Stretching. Balcony. Leaving the apartment. Entering Hideout. Small figure inside large frame.
II — Athletes
Preparation · Effort · Recovery · Community
Spikes on track. Watches. Drills. Then rhythm, repetition, effort. Then hands on knees, walking recovery, conversations. Shoot the full arc — not just the rep.
III — Hideout
Opening · Service · Gathering · Closing
Chairs, lights, first drink, empty room. Then handoff, steam, pouring. Then conversation, reading, waiting. Then sunlight gone, wiped tables, flowers remaining.
IV — Sundays
Setup · Listening · Conversation · Departure
You are not documenting DJs. You are documenting gathering. Selecting records. Adjusting needle. People listening. Flowers. Hands on cups. Not the hero shot.
V — Architecture
Portals · Symmetry · Light · Scale
Build a deliberate archive. You already see architecture differently. Thresholds, corridors, repetition, frames within frames. The strongest recurring signal in your references.
VI — Miami
Rain · Water · Reflections · Morning
The city as atmosphere. Miami after rain is the canonical FIELD frame for a reason. Build a weather archive. Puddles, wet roads, pre-dawn light, bay reflections.
I. Self — Solo Shooting
Tripod · Self-Timer · Presence
The approach

Not portraits of yourself. Presence inside structure. The tripod exists because you are often alone and frequently inside interesting frames that need a small human to complete them.

The mistake is standing and looking at the camera. The correct approach is setting the frame, then entering it and doing the thing you would be doing anyway.

The monk on the bridge didn't pose.
He walked. Someone had the frame ready.
You are both people.
Tripod protocol
Scout firstSet the frame before you're in it. Know exactly where to walk, stop, move.
Timer10-second delay minimum. Burst mode always. Take 20+ frames per setup.
Back to cameraDefault. Anonymous presence is more powerful than portrait here.
Move naturallyWalk past the frame. Run through it. Don't stop and pose inside it.
Review coldLook the next day. The honest frame is rarely the obvious one.
The goalSmall human inside large system. Temple logic, not selfie logic.
II. Athletes — The Full Arc
Track · Long Run · Intervals
Most people shoot
Skip
The rep. The highlight. The finish. The hero moment.
Skip
Athletes looking at camera, posed, aware.
Skip
Action shots that could be stock photography.
FORM shoots
Before — spikes on track, watch setting, drills, the moment before first rep
During — rhythm and repetition over peak effort
After — hands on knees, walking recovery, athletes in conversation
Context — one figure, large environment, weather, architecture of the venue
Logistics
Arrive earlyFind the geometry before the athletes do. The empty track is a frame.
LightPre-dawn intervals or golden hour long runs. Midday is flat.
PositionLow for convergence. Behind for anonymity. Wide for context.
Clip length10–30 seconds. One action, real time. Entrance, movement, exit.
Wet trackWorth waiting for every time. Rain changes everything.
III. Hideout — The Full Day
Interior · Ritual · Community
Rituals most people miss
OpeningEmpty room, chairs, first light, preparation — before anyone arrives.
ServiceThe handoff. Steam. Pour. Cup on wood. Real time, no cuts.
VillageConversation from distance. Reading. Waiting. Communal quiet.
ClosingSunlight disappearing. Wiped tables. Flowers remaining. The after.
Logistics
Arrive earlyShoot the space empty. Then shoot it full. Two completely different images.
Available lightWindow light only where possible. Flash kills the atmosphere every time.
Observer modeMost Hideout images should be taken without directing. Don't ask people to hold still.
ClipsOne action, real time. Pour. Place. Select. No transitions needed. 15–30 seconds.
DetailsFlower arrangement, book spine, cup ring on wood. Accumulated beauty, not arranged beauty.

Camera, lens language, and shot archetypes

Camera & Format
Format (Video)10-bit minimum, 4:2:2 preferred
CodecProRes 422 HQ / H.265 (Long GOP acceptable for web)
Resolution4K (3840×2160) minimum
Frame Rates24fps cinematic / 60fps fluid / 120–240fps slow-mo
Shutter180° rule — 1/48 at 24fps, 1/120 at 60fps
ISOUse base ISO. Expose to the right without clipping.
White Balance5200K–5600K (day) · 3200K–4000K (low light)
FocusManual or AF with intent. Don't hunt.
StabilizationUse support. Don't warp the image.
StillsShoot RAW. Highest dynamic range available.
Lens Language
Focal20mm, 24mm, 35mm, 50mm, 85mm, 135mm
ApertureWide open when appropriate. Depth is a tool.
DistanceGet close. Respect space.
PerspectiveNatural over exaggerated.
MovementLet the shot breathe. Pan and drift with purpose.
The 8 FORM Shot Archetypes
01
Surface Detail
Extreme close — skin, moss, water drops, stone, wood grain. No full subject needed.
02
Subject in Environment
Wide enough to show the world. Body or object is part of landscape, not in front of it.
03
Motion Streak
Slow shutter or slow-mo. Water, limbs, hair — evidence of movement.
04
Reflection
Puddle, glass, goggle, water, mirror. The environment reflected on itself.
05
Hard Light Portrait
Directional sun or source. Shadows on face. Texture revealed. Nothing softened.
06
Backlit Silhouette
Subject against bright sky, water, or window. Luminosity behind, form in front.
07
Compression
Long lens. Subject and environment on one plane. Intimacy and depth.
08
Transition Detail
Hands, feet, objects — the moment between actions. Rest, preparation, arrival.

Camera Profiles

HLG3, Rec.709, and S-Log2 — choose based on the situation. Each has a purpose.

HLG3
Recommended

High dynamic range. Natural contrast. Best for run-and-gun, fast delivery, ready-to-share.

More highlight latitude. Easier than S-Log. Use FORM_HLG LUTs.

Rec.709
Fast Social

Medium dynamic range. Contrasty, punchy. Best for controlled scenes, interviews, social content.

Grade lightly. Apply STILL_Rec709 LUT.

S-Log2
High-End

Maximum latitude. Flat, low contrast. Best for maximum flexibility, grading, and commercial work.

Expose mid to right. Apply SLOG_TO_REC709 LUT base, then grade.

Workflow by Situation
Field / Run & Gun
ProfileHLG3
ExposeTo the right. Apply FIELD_HLG LUT.
Still / Controlled — Interior / Editorial
ProfileRec.709
GradeLightly. Apply STILL_Rec709 LUT.

ProfileS-Log2

Hybrid / Mixed Light
ProfileHLG3
NoteMore forgiving in mixed environments.

Editing & Sound

Rhythm, movement, and music

Editing Rhythm
Hold LengthLet shots breathe. Hold longer than comfortable. Cut on action, not on every beat.
Entry PointUse action to initiate cuts. Cut as subject enters motion.
Exit PointHold 10–15 frames past the action. Let environment settle.
Speed RampsOnly when they add meaning. Slow motion is a punctuation mark, not a filter.
TransitionsHard cuts preferred. Match on motion or texture. No wipes.
Hold ShotsOne long environmental shot per sequence. Let the world exist.
Movement Language
HandheldSTILL: subtle. FIELD: stabilized and controlled.
Push/PullIn. Pull back. Drift sideways. Let the world move around the subject.
ForegroundUse foreground, reflections, and layers whenever available.
No DroneEnvironment establishing only. Never a substitute for ground shots.
No ZoomResist digital zoom entirely. Move the body instead.
Sound & Music Direction
Still/HLGNils Frahm · Ólafur Arnalds · Hania Rani · Johann Johannsson
Field WorldBonobo · Floating Points · Tycho · Boards of Canada
Sound DesignLet sound create space. Silence is a tool. Ambient under music — rain, leaves, water, footsteps.
AvoidDrop-heavy EDM. Generic stock. Anything announcing itself over the image.
Sync StillCut slightly ahead of beat or on breath — not exactly on it.
Sync FieldCut to beat.
Let sound create space.
Silence is a tool.

A sibling document. The perception manual studies the frame — order, atmosphere, capture, color. The cinema doctrine studies people on screen — conduct, ensemble, inference, stillness, service ritual. They meet at director's notes: author the environment, earn the stillness, let ritual reveal character. Vitality under containment. The conversation is never about what it's about.
