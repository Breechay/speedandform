Which running cue should I try?

Choose a cue for something you actually notice. Try it briefly at easy effort and keep it only if it helps without adding strain.

A cue is a handle for an idea.

These are standalone cue options. The six-week Ghost practice has its own structure. Opening this reference does not give you a new weekly assignment.

Try the cue that fits the question.

Practice briefly at the same familiar easy speed.
You noticeTryCheck
Tight hands or shoulders“Soft hands.”Less tension without forcing the arms low.
Reaching or jarring steps“Shorter, easier steps.”Less reaching, without extra calf tension. [1]
Folding forward“Stand a little taller.”Comfortable breathing without a forced back arch.
Movement feels busy“Find the rhythm.”A repeatable pattern without stiffening your trunk.
Effort is rising too far“Ease the pace.”Conversation becomes comfortable again.

Footstep sound does not prove braking. Heel contact is not a diagnosis. Mouth breathing does not mean you have left all aerobic exercise. A cue should help the runner in front of you.

Make a fair comparison.

  1. 01

    Run normally

    Notice the feeling at a familiar easy pace.

  2. 02

    Try one phrase

    Use it for a short stretch without speeding up.

  3. 03

    Return and compare

    Let the cue go. Check whether it helped, then repeat another day.

If a cue adds pain or strain, stop. Persistent or recurring symptoms need assessment. [2]

Use it this week.

Try 1 phrase for 20–30 seconds during a familiar easy run. Return to normal running, compare at the same speed, then check again another day.

Three mistakes to catch.

  1. Using every cue at once.
  2. Forcing silence or nasal breathing.
  3. Keeping a cue that adds pain or strain.

Where does it fit?

A cue is a small experiment inside normal running. It does not replace the amount and effort your session asks for.

The part to take with you

Remember this.

  1. One cue at a time.
  2. A cue is an experiment, not a diagnosis.
  3. Repeat and check before making it a habit.
“Quiet feet” makes you tense your calves to land silently. Should you keep forcing it?

Use less of the cue or leave it. Silence is not the goal if trying to achieve it adds strain.

Try explaining the idea to a friend in your own words.
