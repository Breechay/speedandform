# Same Pace, Different Problem

Owner: Brice. Athletes: Elijah + Simon.
Opened: 2026-10-04.
Public page: `/labs/same-pace-different-problem/`.

## Read this first: the study in 20 seconds

Two runners are aiming near the same half-marathon target. **They should not get the same plan.**

**Elijah races November 14.** He has six weeks. The job is to express the fitness he already has without creating fatigue he cannot recover from.

**Simon races May 16.** He has seven months. The job is to build the runner, then make the selected race pace last for longer and longer distances.

### Theory

Shorter fast work can look good without proving half-marathon durability.

That matters especially for Simon. Brice's working idea is that Simon's bigger problem may be **pacing and durability over distance**, not the ability to run fast for 20–40 minutes.

This is a hypothesis, not a diagnosis.

### How we test it

**Elijah:** 3 × 2 mi → 2 × 3 mi → 3 × 2 mi → 6 mi continuous → taper → Savannah.

The six-mile continuous run is the main check. If it is even, controlled and followed by normal recovery, the target pace stays in the race discussion. If it costs too much, race pace comes down.

**Simon now:** longer controlled Tuesday work + a smaller faster Thursday dose + easy long running.

**Simon later:** distance-based race-pace work: 5 mi → 6 mi → 8 mi → race pace after prior running → 10–12 mi if earned → race.

The pace does not need to get faster every week. The distance held at that pace is the main thing that eventually grows.

### What would change our minds

- If Simon's fresh sustained work is already too costly, the pace is wrong before durability is the problem.
- If Simon keeps his 5K speed with a smaller Thursday dose, a large second quality day is not necessary.
- If Elijah's six-mile run is too costly, sub-1:20 does not become the opening pace just because it was the original goal.

The study is observational. It documents decisions. It does not prove that one plan causes a better result than another.

## Elijah: express existing fitness

Target: **Savannah Half Marathon · November 14, 2026**
Goal: **Sub-1:20 conditional A-goal**

Current interpretation:
- Current 10K best effort from the Oct 3 activity: 39:07 on a bridge-heavy, interrupted route.
- Previous HM: about 1:25.
- Speed is available.
- The short runway makes durability, pacing control, recovery and race execution more important than trying to build a new aerobic ceiling.
- The block should not spend recovery on unnecessary second hard sessions.

Current R4 architecture:
- Weekly load: approximately 32 → 35 → 37 → 33 → 23 → race week.
- Tuesday is the principal specific session.
- Thursday is mostly easy/strides, with only a small sharpening dose where useful.
- Long runs: 10 → 11 → 12 → 10 → 5 miles.
- Main progression:
  - W2: 3 × 2 mi @ 6:08–6:12/mi
  - W3: 2 × 3 mi @ 6:04–6:08/mi if earned
  - W4: 3 × 2 mi @ 6:03–6:08/mi
  - W5: 6 mi continuous @ 6:04–6:08/mi
  - W6: 3 × 1 mi @ 6:04–6:08/mi
- The Oct 27 six-mile continuous session is the primary gate.
- Race pace is selected from the gate plus recovery response.
- Fueling is rehearsed in the longer runs.
- No new large training problem is introduced this close to race day.

Elijah's question:

> **Can the fitness already present be organized into enough continuous race-specific durability to make sub-1:20 a defensible race-day attempt?**

Success before Savannah is not "more fitness everywhere." It is:
- pace that becomes more controlled,
- legs that remain composed,
- recovery that remains normal,
- and a race strategy supported by the evidence.

- The Two Curves remains a coaching model, not a physiological diagnosis.
- HYROX is a competing quality load and must be counted explicitly.

Current R4 Block 01 architecture:
- W2: 64 km
  - Tue 4 × 2 km @ 3:49–3:52/km
  - Thu 4 × 3 min @ 3:33–3:38/km
  - Sat 18 km easy
- W3: 66 km
  - Tue 3 × 3 km @ 3:49–3:52/km
  - Thu 3 × 4 min @ 3:33–3:38/km
  - Sat 19 km easy
- W4: 57 km true absorption
  - Tue 2 × 3 km @ 3:50–3:53/km
  - Thu easy + strides
  - Sat 16 km easy
- W5: 48 km
  - Oct 29 5K field read
  - Sat 12 km easy if recovered
- Gate 01 after normal 5K recovery:
  - standardized 6 km continuous observation near 3:52/km
  - no automatic extension to 8 km during the same test

Simon’s immediate Block 01 question:

> **Can the current working-development pace become more controlled and repeatable while a smaller ceiling dose is retained without consuming the recovery needed for sustainable work?**

But Simon’s longer race-specific authoring direction is **distance based**, borrowing the Race Pace Durability / Speed That Endures model:

> **broken work → 5 mi continuous → 6 mi continuous → 8 mi continuous → race pace after prior running → 10–12 mi → race**

The key principle is:

> **The pace does not automatically get faster because the athlete succeeds. What grows is uninterrupted ownership.**

Later in the season, if the selected race pace is supported by the evidence, the plan may culminate in something close to **12 miles continuous at race pace**, potentially inside a longer run in the spirit of RPD's final 12 inside 16.

That is not a mandatory proof test today and does not guarantee the race. It is the late-stage specificity direction.

Simon's longer-term question:

> **How far can the selected race pace become owned, trusted and durable as the distance grows—and later after prior running?**

## The contrast

### Elijah
**Short runway.**
The job is to express what is already there.

Primary lever:
**specificity + recovery + execution**

We are asking:
**Can he make ~goal pace survive six continuous miles now, then race well six weeks later?**

### Simon
**Long runway.**
The job is to build more capability and then make it durable.

Primary lever:
**months of consistent running + controlled sustained development + later distance ownership**

We are asking:
**How much of his available speed can become owned over 5, 6, 8, 10–12 and eventually 13.1 miles?**

Elijah therefore gets a short race-specific bridge.

Simon eventually gets a true distance ladder.

## What the paired study can teach

The study can make one useful coaching point visible:

> **Same target pace does not mean same plan.**

A pace prescription only makes sense in the context of:
- present fitness,
- previous training,
- race timeline,
- durability,
- pacing behavior,
- recovery,
- competing load,
- and how much uncertainty remains.

The comparison also gives a natural experiment in coaching philosophy:

### Elijah
How much race-specific confidence can be built quickly without overtraining?

### Simon
How much more powerful is the same pace when it has been owned over progressively longer distance for months?

## Paired-study decision points

By Elijah's race, ask:

1. Did Elijah's short specific block make race pace more controlled and durable without creating recovery debt?
2. Did the six-mile gate predict his Savannah execution usefully?
3. Did Simon improve control/continuity while Thursday was reduced?
4. Did Simon's 5K ceiling remain intact despite the smaller secondary dose?
5. Did the paired evidence support the idea that distance ownership is more important than repeatedly proving short-duration speed?

## Study boundaries

This is a coaching study, not a controlled physiological experiment.

Do not claim:
- that a 5K directly measures VO2max,
- that a continuous 6 km run proves HM readiness,
- that one athlete's response causes another athlete's prescription,
- that 12 miles at race pace guarantees 13.1,
- or that one physiological limiter has been isolated from field evidence alone.

The purpose is narrower and more useful:

> **Document how the training decision changes when the athlete, evidence and runway change—even when the desired race pace looks similar.**
