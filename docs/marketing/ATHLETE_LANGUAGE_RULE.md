# Athlete Language Rule

**Decision:** September 15, 2026  
**Applies to:** ads, landing pages, product pages, athlete-facing app copy, onboarding, email, coaching intake, social copy and public plan instructions.

## Core assumption

Do not assume a good or experienced runner knows training language.

A runner may train at a high level, run substantial mileage, complete demanding sessions and still not know abbreviations or coaching concepts that feel obvious to a coach. Fitness literacy and training-literacy are different things.

FORM therefore writes for execution first, understanding second.

> **The coach owns the complexity. The athlete gets a clear action and a clear reason to care.**

## Public-copy rule

An athlete should not have to decode FORM before deciding whether something is useful.

Public copy should usually answer, in this order:

1. **What do I want?** Faster running, a stronger finish, a better race, more consistency, less fading, better fitness.
2. **What do I get?** A plan, coaching, weekly sessions, feedback, a 15-week program, etc.
3. **What will I do?** Run six days, follow the week, complete the workout, build the long run.
4. **What proof is there?** Verified result, completed session, race result, public study, measured progress.
5. **Why does it work?** Method, physiology, durability, threshold, progression and deeper coaching logic come after the benefit is clear.

The first screen, first sentence and first ad frame should normally live in levels 1–3, not level 5.

## No-decoding standard

Prefer language a runner understands without coaching knowledge.

Prefer:

- `warm-up` over `WU`
- `cool-down` over `CD`
- `run your race pace for longer` over `extend race-pace ownership`
- `hold pace late in the race` over `improve late-race durability`
- `5 miles at race pace` over `5-mile continuous ownership rung`
- `one-time payment of $79` over `$79 once`
- `try Weeks 1–4 free` over `inspect the opening four weeks`
- `running coaching in Miami` over a brand phrase that requires explanation

Coach-facing and private technical surfaces can use precise coaching vocabulary. Athlete-facing surfaces may introduce technical terms after the plain-language meaning is already clear.

## Generic is allowed

Do not reject a line merely because it is generic.

A simple line that is instantly understood can outperform a more original line that requires interpretation. Generic language is acceptable when it is:

- true;
- relevant to the actual product;
- specific enough to the desired outcome;
- easy to understand in one pass.

Examples that are acceptable when they fit the offer:

- `Can you keep the pace?`
- `Run faster.`
- `Running coaching in Miami.`
- `Hold your goal pace longer.`
- `Train for a faster half marathon.`

The burden of differentiation can come from the product, visual system, proof and result rather than from clever copy.

## Proof carries the sophistication

Do not force the athlete to understand the thinking in order to trust the work.

The method can stay sophisticated behind a simple surface. Proof should carry much of the credibility:

- verified race results;
- public training sessions;
- before/after performance;
- completed milestones;
- adherence and execution evidence;
- transparent study outcomes.

Explain the method for athletes who want to go deeper, but do not make that understanding a requirement for purchase or execution.

## Ads workflow

For Meta ads, do not begin by polishing FORM doctrine into ad copy.

Use this sequence:

1. write the product truth and hard constraints;
2. let Meta generate multiple plain conversion-oriented alternatives when useful;
3. keep the lines that are immediately understandable;
4. remove anything false, inflated, cheesy, inaccurate or inconsistent with the product;
5. preserve the simplest truthful version;
6. test it.

Meta-generated copy is an ideation source, not an authority. It may invent personalization, guarantees, intensity progression, outcomes or features that do not exist. Those must be rejected.

## Athlete instructions

Every athlete-facing workout or plan surface should be executable by someone who does not know standard running abbreviations.

If an abbreviation is useful, spell it out first. If a term such as threshold, float, strides or race-pace band matters, define it where the athlete encounters it or make the instruction understandable without knowing the term.

Do not make education a prerequisite for compliance.

## Acceptance test

Before publishing athlete-facing copy, ask:

- Could a fit runner with little coaching vocabulary understand this immediately?
- Does the first sentence tell them why they should care?
- Could the same point be said with fewer specialized words?
- Are we asking them to understand the method before showing the benefit?
- Is the proof/result doing enough of the credibility work?
- Would a coach need to explain the copy verbally for it to make sense?

If the last answer is yes, rewrite it.

## Working principle

> **Think deeply backstage. Speak simply out front. Get the athlete fit. Let the result prove the work.**
