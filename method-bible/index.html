01 / FORM

Train to win

I train athletes to win. That is different from simply helping them achieve a goal.

A goal can be a time, a distance, a podium, a return to running, a body that feels capable, or a specific race. Goals matter: they tell us where someone wants to go. But my standard for developing an athlete cannot end at meeting the first target they named.

Training to win asks a deeper question: What kind of athlete would be capable of competing at the highest level available to them, and what must we build to move toward that person?

Winning is a direction for the work, not a promised finish position. An athlete can race brilliantly and finish second. Someone else may never approach the podium yet become faster, stronger, more precise, more resilient, and more competitive than they thought possible. The mindset still changes their preparation.

Training to win is not racing the workouts. It is following the plan, respecting the progression, and executing each session at the effort it was written for. It is doing the work outside the run—strength, mobility, nourishment, sleep, and ordinary recovery—so training days and weeks can stack. It is restraint when restraint protects the training, and commitment when the decisive work arrives.

We train the ability to perform, to learn, to make decisions under pressure, and to bring our best capacity to the moment that asks for it. A session that leaves an athlete unable to execute the next one is not suddenly better because it felt heroic. Winning a training rep is not the goal; being prepared to perform when it matters is the goal.

I care about becoming capable, not merely checking off an event. If we aim at the level required to win, we often develop far beyond the original goal.

Train to win means the right work, at the right effort, in the right place—and the discipline to keep building until it is time to perform.

02 / FORM

The athlete is the work

FORM is a method of Run Development, gait retraining where appropriate, athletic development, and authored programming. It is not a workout generator.

I look for ease, rhythm, timing, control, connection, and what is unnecessarily expensive about the way someone moves. I do not search for a textbook runner to impose on every body. I observe the person in front of me and choose the change that matters most.

An athlete is more than an aerobic number. Development includes movement skill, a stronger engine, speed and power, durable endurance, glute and hip strength, useful range of motion, and the judgment to apply all of it.

The work also includes their habits, environment, confidence, self-talk, and ability to recognize a change in their own body. I want an athlete who can eventually carry the lesson without needing me to repeat the cue.

Reveal what wants to be set free. Not everything needs fixing. Some things just cost you more.

The qualities we develop

  • Movement and skill: posture, rhythm, arm-and-leg timing, landing, and the ability to repeat useful mechanics.
  • Aerobic capacity: doing more useful work with a lower relative demand, and recovering for the next exposure.
  • Speed and power: having faster movement available than the race immediately demands.
  • Durability: keeping form and pace after meaningful work has accumulated.
  • The frame: glutes, hips, hamstrings, calves, feet, trunk, back, shoulders, and their ability to produce and absorb force.
  • Fluidity: organized tension where it supports the movement and freedom where it needs to move.
  • Ownership: recognizing an effort, managing decisions, and knowing when an instruction is useful.

These abilities develop on different clocks. Aerobic improvements can arrive before the body is ready for more impact. A cue can feel right once before it becomes a stable skill. I do not mistake one good afternoon for a finished adaptation.

03 / FORM

What I count as evidence

My direct observations, what an athlete reports, and how that athlete actually progresses are legitimate grounds for coaching decisions. I do not require a published study or a particular sample size before adopting an idea I have tested and judged useful in practice.

I want to know where an idea came from. I keep separate:

  1. What actually happened: the repetition, split, distance, load, or training result.
  2. What the athlete said: their sensation, concern, understanding, or description.
  3. What I observed: the movement, behavior, response, or pattern I saw.
  4. How I interpreted it: the explanation I think is most useful.
  5. What I changed: a cue, exercise, dose, or programming decision.
  6. What later made me revise it.

Other coaches, research, and accepted methods are valuable comparisons. They are not the only places good coaching knowledge originates. I can be confident enough to act while remaining willing to change.

That is not permission to ignore pain, clinical restrictions, contrary evidence, or consent. A useful coaching cue is also not automatically proof of a universal biomechanical mechanism. The origin of the idea and the claim I make about it must match.

What I observe and what my athletes tell me can become a FORM method. When the evidence changes, the method changes.

04 / FORM

Purpose, dose, and recovery cost

Every session must earn its place in the week. I distinguish three questions that are too often blended together.

Skill acquisition

What is the athlete learning? Is this repetition helping them feel timing, rhythm, landing, posture, or control? How much high-quality practice is needed for a meaningful lesson?

Training stimulus

What adaptation are we trying to create? What duration, intensity, repetition count, and recovery actually deliver that stimulus for this athlete today?

Recovery cost

What does the session consume afterward: muscularly, mechanically, metabolically, neurologically, and mentally? What does that take away from Thursday, Saturday, strength, and the next decisive workout?

The right dose is the appropriate sufficient dose. Not always small. A difficult race-specific session can be exactly right. But the next repetition needs a reason.

What does another rep buy, and what does it cost?

During my own 20 × 200 m session, I learned something valuable about arm rhythm and the way running beside others helped me calibrate timing. The learning mattered. That does not mean twenty repetitions was the best dose for acquiring that skill inside an already demanding training week. Next time a shorter practice could preserve the lesson and leave more capacity for the work ahead.

Fatigue is real evidence of cost, not a medal. Finishing a workout does not prove that the intended adaptation has been established.

05 / FORM

Author the whole week, not isolated highlights

The week is the unit of judgment. Before writing an impressive Tuesday, Thursday, or Saturday, I ask what this athlete needs to develop now and what must remain available for that development.

I study recent actual training, reliable habits, the goal and competitive demands, running history, available days, outside training, current strength work, limitations, and the athlete's own reports.

Then I write the full executable progression: the key sessions, easy support, appropriate rest, total whole-run distances or durations, pacing or effort, recoveries, step-back periods, and taper where needed.

No artificial pending-review gates. An authored progression advances as written. The athlete can text me or file in FORM; when new evidence changes the right plan, I revise the future work deliberately. I do not withhold next week merely because a routine check-in has not happened.

More running days can distribute aerobic work usefully without adding more hard days. A strength day and a fast-finish long run both spend recovery. An easy twelve-mile run may be gentle in intensity and still have a meaningful mechanical cost.

I sometimes stack demanding work deliberately to preserve genuine easy days afterward. That is a strategy, not a universal rule. The stress must buy something important and not spoil the next principal session.

The athlete does not get extra credit for turning easy running into threshold, or training through exhaustion to feel dedicated. Eating, sleeping, showing up prepared, and staying consistent are part of the competitive plan. The purpose is to stack useful adaptations, not collapse after isolated big days.

A clear week is better than seven impressive workout cards.

06 / FORM

Easy running makes everything else possible

Easy running builds the engine, supports repeatable practice, and helps an athlete return to more demanding work. It is not a failed workout or time that must be filled with extra speed.

Easy is an actual effort, not simply a pace printed on a card. If a nominal easy pace feels like seven or eight out of ten for the runner today, it is not functioning as easy work. Run the effort. Let the pace appear.

Ten useful minutes count. Twenty or thirty may be the right dose. More mileage matters when it helps the larger development and can be absorbed. Frequency is valuable because the athlete can practice often without every exposure asking for substantial recovery.

The trampoline

I describe threshold as the bounce of a trampoline. Easy running builds the legs that support it. The visible height depends on the structure underneath. This is a teaching image, not a literal claim about anatomy.

Deposits and withdrawals

Some runs are deposits. They build capacity at relatively low cost and help make future training possible. Others are withdrawals. They spend more recovery to purchase an important adaptation or rehearsal.

Withdrawals are not bad. The problem is spending the same recovery twice. Easy days build what hard days draw from.

07 / FORM

Threshold: learn a pace you can inhabit

Threshold is controlled, sustainable high-end aerobic work, not a race rehearsal disguised as training. I want a runner to enter a demanding rhythm, stay composed, and become able to sustain more work without losing organization.

Broken repetitions and continuous efforts can train different things. We may grow total time, the longest uninterrupted segment, continuity, density, or how late the work arrives. Faster splits are not the only mark of progress.

The runner's experience matters. Someone may find continuous threshold easier to inhabit and repetitions harder because the restarting disrupts their rhythm. Another may benefit from the breaks. I use what we learn to choose the tool.

The pace stays. The escapes disappear. We gradually need fewer breaks to express the same capability.

Good threshold training teaches the athlete to remain strong and controlled, not to win an ordinary training session.

08 / FORM

Speed reserve: build more than the race needs

Have more speed to draw from as we build the endurance to sustain it.

I train speed deliberately. I want the athlete to experience quicker movement, become familiar with it, repeat it with good rhythm, and eventually sustain a greater fraction of that speed for longer.

That can begin with short, fully controlled 100- or 200-meter exposures and generous recovery. As the athlete develops, it can progress to 400s, 600s, 800s, short work-and-float intervals, longer repetitions, and sustained race-specific work. Those are tools, not a mandatory universal sequence.

Two hundred meters is not one workout. A 200 can teach rhythm, touch speed, sharpen a practiced gear, or create a demanding physical stimulus. Repetition count and rest have to answer which of those jobs we intend.

Advanced athletes may need a much larger stimulus; newer runners often need clearer repetitions before more repetitions. Training should make them more capable, not merely expose how hard they can push today.

Build the top end. Then teach the athlete to hold more of it.

09 / FORM

Long runs have different jobs

The long run is not always the same easy distance with a larger number.

  • Aerobic long run: genuinely easy duration, fueling opportunity, and time on feet.
  • Progression or steady finish: mostly easy work with a deliberate rise in effort.
  • Race-specific long run: meaningful pace work after earlier accumulated duration or distance.
  • Step-back or taper: less stress so earlier training can be absorbed and expressed.

Some long runs in a race-focused block need to be actual workouts. Not all of them. A fast finish consumes the week's quality budget and cannot be treated as a free addition to easy mileage.

The progression may be more distance, but it may instead be better continuity, a stronger final portion, better fuel and execution, or the ability to bring useful speed later into the run.

Keep the speed. Move it deeper.

Race demands change the plan

The 5K needs repeatable speed, aerobic power, efficient fast movement, and speed above race requirement. The 10K needs threshold control and the ability to move among gears. The half marathon asks whether pace remains available after meaningful earlier work. The marathon needs progressively more consequential long-run architecture, fuel practice, and late-session execution. HYROX adds the real cost of stations and transitions; that does not excuse neglecting the runner.

The event changes the question. It does not change my insistence on an individual dose and a coherent week.

10 / FORM

Strength belongs in the athlete

I always develop the glutes. Glutes and hips are central to my concept of a capable athlete, whether that person is training primarily for running, speed, strength, physique, or a combination.

Universal priority does not mean identical exercise or dose. Existing restrictions, readiness, the athlete's experience, and running workload determine how that development happens.

The full frame matters too: hamstrings, calves, feet, quads, trunk, back, shoulders and useful upper-body strength. I want athletes who can produce force, receive it, stay coordinated, and remain capable late in demanding work.

Strength has its own adaptations. It is not only background injury prevention, and it should not be described as inherently opposed to power. But it must be programmed with the running week. A hard lower-body lift can change Thursday speed; a hard Saturday long run can change the value of lifting on Sunday.

For athletes using FORM Running and Forge Strength, I review both the current week and following seven days when making a meaningful adjustment. Each system retains its own training authority. I do not invent a strength prescription inside a running record.

11 / FORM

The bow: tension and fluidity

I picture the athletic body as a bow. It needs tension where it holds and range where it moves. Tight everywhere and it cannot express the movement. Loose everywhere and it cannot organize force. The value is in the relationship.

That is why I care about fluidity and why I use Yin yoga and mobility. I want healthy, strong athletes who can move without unnecessary resistance and stay relaxed where relaxation is useful.

Yin is one option, not a magical replacement for strength or rehabilitation. The appropriate mobility dose depends on the athlete and the movement. My own personal mobility goals are not automatically everybody's prescription.

Strong where it holds. Free where it moves. Responsive in return.

12 / FORM

Drills, plyometrics, and teaching movement

FORM will keep building its own curriculum of drills, elastic work, rhythm practice, and plyometrics. Those methods have to be authored and progressed, not assigned wholesale because they sound athletic.

Each drill needs a purpose, a quality target, a sufficient number of exposures, and a cost. A movement cue should be judged by what it changes in the athlete, not whether it resembles a perfect-looking demonstration.

How I teach a cue

  1. DO: a short action the athlete can execute while moving.
  2. THINK OF IT LIKE THIS: a tactile image, rhythm, or analogy.
  3. WHY: a brief explanation if it makes the action easier to understand.

Sometimes running alongside another athlete gives a rhythm reference. The athlete can borrow that timing and gradually own it. Sometimes moving the arms farther back is an effective feeling for a particular runner. That is a cue to examine, not a claim that every runner should change their arms the same way.

The athlete carries the cue. The coach carries the model.

13 / FORM

The mental athlete is still an athlete

The mind is in the session. Attention, patience, self-talk, confidence, frustration, and choice affect how athletes execute work and compete.

I coach those things. Stop negotiating with yourself is one of my cues for an athlete whose safe, appropriate challenge is being interrupted by unnecessary bargaining. It is never permission to ignore pain, alarming symptoms, or an unsuitable workload.

I want a runner to recognize when a demanding movement becomes organized, almost meditative. The ability to stay with effort and the ability to back off for the right reason are both forms of judgment.

Training to win includes thinking like a competitor without confusing recklessness for courage.

14 / FORM

Every athlete is a living study

I treat the development of each athlete as an ongoing study. That does not mean turning their private details into public content.

I preserve the goal, the actual training, the athlete's words, my observations, the result, the cost, and what I concluded. I keep what is measured separate from what is reported and what I infer. One person's case can teach me something important without pretending it proves the same result in everyone.

Useful running baselines include approximate average and best reported or filed performances at 200, 400, 600, 800, and 1,000 meters. A personal report does not need an official race certificate to be useful in coaching. It does need honest attribution.

New observations can change a future prescription. They should not rewrite the history of what was actually performed.

15 / FORM

The app is the instrument, not the coach

FORM Running should make sophisticated coaching simple to execute. The athlete opens a Week and understands the session: exact dose, distance or duration, effort or pace, repetitions, recovery, warm-up, cooldown, and a useful reason.

The coach sees the deeper model. The app preserves versions, evidence, athlete reporting, plan decisions, and appropriate sharing. A recorded workout should match what happened, not what was supposed to happen; the athlete's Week and member feed should tell the same factual story under the athlete's sharing preferences.

A clean interface is a coaching decision too. Do not repeat the structured prescription as a second paragraph. Do not display conflicting units or a stale weekly explanation. Do not confuse a saved edit with a published plan, an email with an in-app notice, or a database entry with an installed native release.

Agent assistance is welcome. Agents should read the current sources, surface discrepancies, help me author, and verify actual outcomes. They do not silently replace my decisions or invent missing evidence.

16 / FORM

The authoring test

Before writing a plan, I ask:

  1. Who is this athlete, and what would training to win mean in their actual context?
  2. What do their recent training, words, and movement tell me?
  3. What capability should change first?
  4. Where is glute development, and what other strength is being trained?
  5. Where is useful mobility and fluidity?
  6. Which session matters most this week, and what must stay easy to protect it?
  7. What does the long run do: aerobic support, progression, specific work, or step-back?
  8. Does each demanding repetition buy skill, a stimulus, or a worthwhile lesson?
  9. What outside work consumes the athlete's recovery budget?
  10. Is the progression authored all the way through, including sensible reductions?
  11. Are the pace prescriptions and baseline marks actually attributable?
  12. Will the athlete understand the work, and will filing preserve what happened?
  13. What observation could change my mind later?

The answer becomes a complete training plan, not a set of impressive cards. When new evidence requires a change, I make it explicitly and preserve the previous version.

17 / FORM

How this bible changes

This is a living public account of my method, not a fixed claim of universal scientific truth. I will add field observations, new teaching language, and refined training rules as practice teaches me.

An adopted method should record what prompted it, where it applies, what it helps, and what later revised it. Other coaches and published work can challenge or inform the idea. The athlete's actual response stays central.
