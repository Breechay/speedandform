How fast should my long run be?

Most long runs start at an easy, conversational effort, for a duration you already handle. A faster finish belongs only when your plan and recovery support it.

Time adds weight to the run.

The long run practices keeping a manageable effort going as time passes. Research shows that responses can change during prolonged running and differ between runners. [1]

You also learn practical things: starting patiently, choosing a route, drinking, eating and finishing in control. Those skills matter on race day.

Choose the job before the pace.

Two possible long-run structures.
StructureWhat you doWhen it fits
All easyKeep conversation comfortable.A familiar starting choice, especially when extending time.
Easy, then steadyStart easy, then use a planned stronger finish below threshold effort.When the duration is familiar and the wider week supports extra effort.

Steady means more purposeful than easy, while still controlled. There is no fixed number of seconds slower than threshold that makes every runner’s long run right.

How long should you run?

Begin near a duration you recently handled comfortably. Repeat it, check your recovery, then decide whether to extend it. A percentage rule is not a safety guarantee. [5]

If your current longer run is 30 minutes, do not jump to an hour because of this example. The right duration depends on your recent week, goal and recovery.

Does the finish need to be fast?

No. Finishing an easy long run easily is a completed job.

A stronger finish is planned and you still feel controlled.
Ease into the assigned effort. Stop at the planned amount.
You feel good, but today’s plan is all easy.
Keep it easy. You can finish feeling good.
Breathing or movement is getting harder to control.
Drop the faster part. Slow down or shorten the run. Review the whole run afterward.

Use training as the dress rehearsal.

Know where you can drink. On longer outings, practice familiar food or sports products rather than saving your first experiment for race day. The amount depends on time, effort and what your stomach handles. [3] Read the fueling guide →

In heat, adjust the time of day, route, pace or duration. [4] If pain changes your stride, stop; recurring pain needs assessment. [2]

Afterward, record the time, how the finish felt, what you ate or drank, and how the next day felt. The next run tells you something too.

Questions before the next long run.

Must I run 13.1 miles before a half marathon?

No single training distance is required for everyone. Recent running, time on feet and recovery matter. Completing race distance in training is not a universal entry test.

Must the long run be on Saturday?

Choose a day that gives the run and recovery enough room. The weekday is a scheduling choice.

Does training only start working after a certain minute?

No. The whole run contributes. There is no magic minute when the earlier running suddenly starts to count.

Use it this week.

Use the duration you recently handled. If 60 minutes is already familiar, the 10 + 40 + 10-minute example below keeps the entire run easy. Record the finish and how you feel the next day.

Three mistakes to catch.

  1. Adding time and a fast finish together.
  2. Copying someone else’s longest run.
  3. Saving your first food or drink experiment for race day.

Where does it fit?

The long run extends time on your feet. Pace control and food-and-drink practice help you use that endurance on race day.

The part to take with you

Remember this.

  1. Longer is already harder.
  2. Add time or add effort. Be careful about adding both.
  3. Practice the pacing, food and drink you will need.
You want to extend your long run and add a hard finish this week. How many changes is that?

Two: more time and more effort. First establish the longer easy run. Add a faster part only when your plan and recovery support it.

Try explaining the idea to a friend in your own words.
