The running library · 03 / The long run

Run farther without racing the distance.

Most long runs begin at an easy, conversational effort. Choose a duration that fits your recent training. A faster finish is an optional training choice, not the price of a good long run.

By Brice IkouebeUpdated September 17, 2026

Long is a demand of its own.

A run does not need to be fast to be demanding. More time on your feet changes its cost. Research shows that physiological responses can change as running continues, and that trained and less-trained runners do not respond identically. [1]

The long run gives you practice holding a manageable effort for longer, making pacing decisions, and learning what food, drink, and equipment work for you. The whole run matters. Adaptation does not switch on at minute 50.

Steady is more purposeful than easy, without becoming threshold work. No fixed number of seconds slower than threshold identifies the right long-run pace for every athlete. Use your established running, the purpose of the day, and your response.

Start with a duration you already handle.

Begin near a duration you have recently completed comfortably. Repeat it before extending it. Do not increase distance and add a harder finish in the same step. Gradual increases and repeated exposure are more sensible than treating a weekly percentage as a safety guarantee. [2]

If 30 minutes is currently your longer run, there is no need to jump to an hour. If you already handle much longer runs, the next step depends on your race and the rest of your week. This is a way to choose, not a universal mileage prescription.

Make the finish a decision, not a dare.

The run is comfortable and the plan says easy.
Keep it easy. Feeling good does not require an upgrade.
A steady finish was planned and you still feel controlled.
Move up gradually. Keep the finish within its assigned effort and duration; do not chase a best split.
Breathing keeps rising or your stride starts to deteriorate.
Drop the faster finish, slow down, or shorten the run. Review pace, duration, conditions, and fueling afterward instead of diagnosing the cause mid-run.
You started too fast.
Slow down now. The start cannot be undone, but the remaining run can still be adjusted. One mistake does not mean the session or your aerobic system is damaged.

Practice the practical things.

On longer outings, plan access to drink and take the fuel you intend to practice with. Use familiar choices and learn during training rather than making race day the first attempt. Nutrition needs depend on the duration, intensity, conditions, and your tolerance. [3]

In heat, adjust timing, route, pace, and duration. Do not turn an easy-effort run into a heat test. [4] Stop if pain changes your stride; persistent or recurring pain needs assessment. [2]

Afterward, note the duration, how the finish felt, what you ate or drank, and how you recovered. Use the next day’s response when deciding whether to repeat or extend the run.

Questions that come up.

Do I need a fast finish every week?

No. An all-easy long run has a clear job. A faster finish adds intensity to duration and needs room in the wider training week.

Does the long run have to be on Saturday?

No. Choose a day that gives the run and your recovery enough space. Keep a useful routine without treating a weekday as a training adaptation.

Should a half-marathon long run be 13.1 miles?

There is no single required distance for every runner. Choose from recent volume, time on feet, race expectations, and recovery. Completing race distance in training is not a prerequisite for every first half marathon.
