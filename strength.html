The running library · 06 / Strength for runners

Get stronger. Keep running well.

Train your legs and trunk with movements you can control, then gradually make them more demanding. The right dose leaves room for your running.

By Brice IkouebeUpdated September 17, 2026

Strength is more than a warm-up.

Strength work develops the ability to produce force. In research, adding heavy strength work to endurance training has improved running economy: the oxygen needed at a given pace. That is a measured outcome in studied runners, not a promise that any exercise circuit will make every runner faster. [1]

I use strength to build useful capacity alongside running. That can include lifting, single-leg work, calf work, and, when appropriate, faster or springier movements. A few activation drills and a progressive strength program do different jobs.

Cover the basics before adding variety.

01

Squat or split squat

Bend and straighten the knee and hip under control. A chair or hand support can help you learn the movement.

02

Hip hinge

Push the hips back, then stand tall. Try a light Romanian deadlift or a bridge that you can repeat comfortably.

03

Calf raise

Rise and lower through a controlled range. Start with two feet and hand support when needed.

04

Trunk control

Use a carry or a comfortable side-plank variation. Keep breathing; do not brace so hard that you cannot.

These are movement options, not diagnoses. A wobble, a posture, or a video score alone does not establish a weak muscle or tell us the cause of pain. Read the running-form guide →

Start small enough to learn.

Start with one session in a week when the load is new. Build toward two if it fits your goal and recovery. Muscle effort is expected; sharp pain or joint pain is a reason to stop and change the exercise. Get qualified help with unfamiliar lifts or recurring symptoms.

Progress the work, not the soreness.

Once the movement is familiar, the last repetitions should require effort without turning into a different movement. When all sets feel controlled and the following runs remain manageable, increase the load a little or add a set. Do not change everything at once.

Already experienced with lifting?

Light circuits may not provide the stimulus you need. Heavier sets and appropriately introduced explosive work can have a role. The exercise, load, and number of sets should reflect your lifting experience and running priorities, not a blanket instruction to stay light. The research example involved trained runners and supervised heavy half-squats; it is not a novice starting program. [1]

Do I need to feel sore?

No. Soreness is not your score. Track the load, repetitions, control, and how your next runs feel. Novel or excessive work that repeatedly disrupts running is a reason to reduce the dose, not proof that the workout was effective.

Protect the run that matters.

Do not introduce a hard leg session the day before a race, long run, or important workout. Familiar lifting can still affect the following day’s running. Small experimental studies show that the order and load of combined training matter; they do not establish one perfect timetable for everyone. [2]

The next run feels normal.
Keep the dose stable and look for progress over several weeks.
Soreness keeps changing your stride.
Reduce sets or load, move the session, and leave more recovery. Do not force the planned run through altered movement.
You are racing soon.
Use familiar work at a reduced dose or leave it out. Save new exercises and ambitious progressions for another week.
Sources & context

The examples and decisions above are FORM coaching guidance. The research below supports the stated principles, not an individual prescription or a guaranteed result.

  1. Støren et al., 2008Maximal strength training and running economySeventeen well-trained runners were randomized. Eight weeks of heavy half-squats improved measured running economy in the intervention group. The small sample and specific protocol limit generalization.
  2. Doma and Deakin, 2013Order of strength and endurance trainingAn acute study of combined sessions and next-day running. It supports attention to fatigue and sequence, not a universal number of recovery hours.
