What makes running training work?

Do useful work, recover, then return to it. Add the challenge your running can support over time, with a week you can repeat.

Think of learning an instrument.

A useful week is one you can build on. Consistency does not mean ignoring illness, pain or life. Adjusting is part of keeping the practice going. [1]

Give each run one clear job.

An easy run gives you manageable endurance practice. Threshold practices strong sustained running. A long run extends time. A session does not need to do everything.

If the job is easy running, a fast finish is not bonus credit. It is a different demand. A substantial amount of easier work belongs in endurance training. [2]

Leave room for the next piece.

Food, sleep and space between demanding sessions are parts of training. More recovery gadgets cannot repair a week that keeps asking for too much. [3]

Hard work can belong in a plan. Exhaustion is not the score. Judge the session by the job it did and whether you can recover from it.

Move one dial at a time.

Longer runs, more days, faster pace and shorter rests all change the work. Adding all four makes it harder to know what helped or what became too much.

For example, if a familiar easy run grows a little longer, keep its effort easy. Repeat and review before changing something else. No weekly percentage guarantees safety. [4]

Practice what the goal will ask.

A half marathon asks you to keep going. That makes sustained running, pacing and longer-run practice relevant. Short quick running can help a different part of preparation, but winning a short repetition does not prove you can hold a race pace for 13.1 miles.

Different races ask different things. Choose training from the runner you are now and the task ahead. [1]

Learn, then choose the next step.

  1. 01

    Name the job

    What should this session teach?

  2. 02

    Notice the result

    What did you do, feel and recover from?

  3. 03

    Choose

    Repeat, extend, shorten or change the format.

A change that helps your friend is a question to investigate, not an automatic instruction for you. Keep what helps your running.

Use it this week.

Review your last 3 runs. For each, name the job and how you recovered. Choose 1 useful change, or repeat what is working, rather than increasing time, speed and frequency together.

Three mistakes to catch.

  1. Treating exhaustion as proof of progress.
  2. Changing several demands without checking the response.
  3. Copying another runner’s progression without checking your starting point.

Where does it fit?

These principles connect every other lesson: useful practice, manageable demand, recovery and a next step chosen from your response.

The part to take with you

Remember this.

  1. Repeat useful work.
  2. Recover enough to build on it.
  3. Change the plan when the evidence asks.
You repeat last week’s workout and it feels controlled again. Is repeating it wasted time?

No. Familiar work can keep doing its job. Look at control, recovery and the goal before deciding that the calendar needs something harder.

Try explaining the idea to a friend in your own words.

Sources & context

The examples and decisions above are FORM coaching guidance. The research below supports the stated principles, not an individual prescription or a guaranteed result.

  1. Jack DanielsThe basic laws of running, from Daniels’ Running FormulaCoaching context: understand each workout’s purpose, adjust to the individual, and include food and rest.
  2. Esteve-Lanao et al. (2007)Impact of training intensity distribution on performance in endurance athletesA small study in subelite runners supports substantial easier training, not a universal percentage.
  3. Meeusen et al. (2013)ECSS and ACSM consensus on training, recovery and overtrainingExcessive load and inadequate recovery need attention; a watch cannot diagnose the cause of persistent symptoms.
  4. Buist et al. (2008)No effect of a graded training program on running-related injuries in novice runnersA novice-runner trial did not find injury reduction from its program based on the 10% rule. No weekly percentage is a safety guarantee.
