The running library · 05 / Your training week

Build a week you can repeat.

Start with the days you can reliably train. Give the important sessions room, keep easy running easy, and leave space to recover.

By Brice IkouebeUpdated September 17, 2026

Choose the work. Then give it room.

A useful week has a purpose, not just a mileage total. In an established FORM endurance block, I often build around a controlled threshold session and a long run. Easy runs build endurance around them. They are training in their own right. [1]

That is a coaching framework, not a rule that everyone needs two demanding days. Starting out, returning after time away, or already struggling to recover? Build a comfortable running routine before adding threshold work. A longer run can be demanding even when its pace is easy.

Four runs. One clear rhythm.

Tuesday and Saturday are useful anchors, not sacred dates. Move the pattern around your life. As a starting arrangement, put easier or rest days between demanding sessions and avoid unfamiliar heavy leg work immediately before a key run. Adjust from your actual response, not a fixed recovery clock. [2]

Fewer days does not mean cramming more in.

Three runs available

Start with an easy run, another easy run or an established controlled workout, and a longer easy run. For example: Tuesday, Thursday, and Sunday. Keep the longer run within a duration you already handle. Do not turn all three days into tests.

Five runs available

Keep the same priorities. Add a short easy run to a day you recover well on, rather than adding a second hard workout by default. An extra available day is not proof that you need more load.

Starting or returning

Use short, comfortable runs or run-walk sessions, with rest between them as needed. First establish what you tolerate consistently. Do not copy the threshold day above simply because it is Tuesday.

These are examples of placement, not complete training plans. Volume, pace, and progression still depend on your starting point.

Strength belongs in the same plan.

Count lifting as training, not an invisible extra. A challenging lower-body session, hills, a hard group run, and a long run all contribute to the week’s load. Do not judge the week by the running calendar alone.

Start with a small strength dose you can recover from. Some runners combine familiar lifting with a running day to keep other days lighter. Others need separate days. Protect the session that matters most and adjust if lifting changes your stride or leaves the next key run flat. See strength for runners →

When the week changes, change the week.

A workout gets missed.
Do not stack it beside the next demanding session just to complete the checklist. Drop it or move it only when there is still room to recover.
Work, travel, or poor sleep takes more out of you.
Reduce the week’s demands. Keep a few manageable runs rather than forcing the original plan into less time.
The same sessions keep feeling worse.
Review the whole load, food, sleep, and symptoms. Persistent fatigue or declining performance deserves assessment, not another harder session. [2]
The week feels manageable.
Repeat it before adding more. When progressing, choose one clear change, such as a little more easy time, rather than increasing frequency, duration, and intensity together.
Sources & context

The examples and decisions above are FORM coaching guidance. The research below supports the stated principles, not an individual prescription or a guaranteed result.

  1. Esteve-Lanao et al., 2007Training intensity distribution in endurance runnersA small randomized study in subelite runners supports substantial lower-intensity work. It does not validate this exact calendar or a universal training ratio.
  2. Meeusen et al., 2013ECSS and ACSM consensus on training, recovery and overtrainingA clinical consensus about excessive load and inadequate recovery. Persistent symptoms have multiple possible causes; a calendar or watch score cannot diagnose them.
