Not everything needs fixing.

Start with something that is costing you effort, comfort, or control. Change one thing, then compare. A movement does not need correcting just because it looks different.

By Brice IkouebeUpdated September 17, 2026

Start with the runner, not the screenshot.

Ask what you feel and when it appears. Tension from the first minute is a different coaching question from a rhythm that changes late in a hard run. A video helps you observe movement; it does not diagnose weakness, injury, or running economy by itself.

  1. 01

    Notice

    Name a specific feeling or difficulty. Observe it at a known pace.

  2. 02

    Try

    Use one small cue at easy effort. Keep the comparison fair.

  3. 03

    Check

    Does it feel easier or more repeatable without a new problem?

A useful change should survive more than one promising minute. Recheck it on another day before treating it as established.

Find the observation that fits.

These are starting points for a coaching experiment, not diagnoses. Leave comfortable, repeatable movement alone.

You feel as though each step brakes.

Notice: Is the sensation still present when you slow down? A foot landing ahead of you in a side-view image is not, by itself, proof of a problem.

Try: “Shorter, easier steps.” Keep the speed easy instead of speeding up to increase your step rate. Small step-rate increases changed several measures of knee and hip loading in a laboratory study; that is not proof of injury prevention or better performance for every runner. [1]

Check: Less reaching or jarring, without extra calf tension. Do not force the foot directly under your hips.

One side feels less controlled.

Notice: When does it appear, and does it persist at an easier pace? A visible hip drop does not establish that a particular muscle is weak or “not firing.”

Try: Reduce the pace first. Use a simple cue such as “tall and balanced” rather than locking the pelvis rigid.

Check: Whether the movement feels more controlled and repeatable. Persistent pain or a new limp belongs with a qualified clinician, not a guessed exercise diagnosis. [4]

You are worried about landing on your heel.

Notice: Heel contact is a description, not a diagnosis. Do not switch your foot strike just to resemble another runner.

Understand: Different foot-strike patterns distribute load differently. In a study of habitual patterns, forefoot runners had lower kneecap-joint loading but greater Achilles-tendon loading. The comparison does not show that switching prevents injury. [2]

Check: Comfort and control at your normal easy pace. A new foot strike is not automatically a better one.

Your arms feel busy or your torso twists.

Notice: Some arm crossover and trunk rotation are part of running. The reason to investigate is a felt cost, not a line drawn down a video.

Try: Relax your hands and let the arms swing naturally. Avoid pinning your elbows to your sides or forcing a rigid forward-back track.

Check: Less tension without having to keep policing every arm swing.

You feel as though you are bouncing upward.

Notice: Running includes vertical movement. A watch’s bounce value is not an objective grade of your form.

Try: At easy speed, stop deliberately pushing upward. A slightly shorter step is another small experiment when reaching is part of the same pattern. [1]

Check: A smoother feeling without crouching, shuffling, or tensing your legs. The lowest bounce is not the target.

You fold forward as you tire.

Notice: Compare early and later running at similar speeds. Do not infer that your glutes have stopped working from the position of your torso.

Try: “Stand a little taller.” Let the ribs settle comfortably over the pelvis without arching your back or forcing an ankle lean.

Check: Easier breathing and control. When the posture takes constant effort to hold, reduce the training demand instead of bracing harder.

Your shoulders or hands keep tightening.

Notice: Is the tension present from the start, or does it appear as effort rises?

Try: Unclench the hands and let the shoulders soften. Allow your normal arm height rather than pushing the hands toward the hips.

Check: Less tension at the same easy speed. If the cue needs constant effort, use less of it or leave it.

Cadence is a setting, not a score.

Cadence is your steps per minute, counting both feet. There is no universal 180-step requirement. At a given speed, runners often choose a step frequency close to their economical range; a number alone is not a reason to change it. [3]

When a step-rate experiment is appropriate, begin with your natural cadence and make a small change. For example, 5% above 160 is 168, not 180. The arithmetic is an illustration, not your prescription. Keep speed comparable and stop when the change adds strain. [1]

Open the practice metronome

Choose the cadence you intend to practice. One beat is one step.

Choose 100–220 steps per minute. Plays for up to 60 seconds.

Make the comparison useful.

A useful observation

“At the same easy speed, my shoulders felt less tense with that cue.”

More than the evidence says

“This cue fixed my form and made me more efficient.”

Illustrative statements, not an athlete testimonial.

Separate what the runner reports, what you can see, what a device measures, and the decision you make. Heart rate during a brief comparison is not a direct measurement of running economy.

Try one cue for a short stretch, return to your usual running, then compare again. Do not rehearse a new pattern through pain. Stop and seek assessment for persistent or recurring pain, especially when it changes how you run. [4]

Sources & context

The examples and decisions above are FORM coaching guidance. The research below supports the stated principles, not an individual prescription or a guaranteed result.

  1. Heiderscheit et al. (2011)Effects of step rate manipulation on joint mechanics during runningLaboratory changes at a fixed speed in 45 healthy runners. Loading effects are not the same as demonstrated injury prevention.
