Strength & movement · Lesson 07

What should I change in my running form?

Start with something that costs you effort, comfort or control. Try one small change and check whether it helps; looking different alone does not tell you what needs changing.

Tune one string at a time.

Ask what feels difficult and when it appears. Tight shoulders from the start and a stride that changes late in a long run are different questions.

  1. 01

    Notice

    Name a feeling or difficulty at a familiar pace.

  2. 02

    Try

    Use one small cue at easy effort.

  3. 03

    Check

    Did it feel better without causing another problem?

Check again on another day. One promising minute is a reason to practice, not proof that the job is finished.

Choose the question that fits.

Each step feels like it brakes.

Try “shorter, easier steps” at the same easy speed. Check for less reaching or jarring without extra calf tension. A foot ahead of you in one image is not proof of a problem. Small step-rate changes can alter joint loading, but that does not prove injury prevention. [1]

One side feels less controlled.

Slow down first. Try “tall and balanced,” without locking your hips rigid. Check whether control improves. A visible hip drop does not diagnose a weak muscle. Persistent pain or a new limp needs assessment. [4]

I land on my heel. Is that bad?

Heel contact is a description. Different foot strikes distribute load differently, including between the knee and Achilles tendon. Switching is not automatically better. Start with comfort and control rather than copying another runner. [2]

My arms feel busy.

Unclench your hands and let the arms swing naturally. Check for less tension. Some crossing and twisting are normal; you do not need to force your elbows into a straight track.

I feel like I bounce upward.

Stop deliberately pushing upward. A slightly shorter step can be an experiment if reaching is part of the feeling. Running naturally includes some up-and-down motion. The lowest watch value is not the goal. [1] See cadence practice →

I fold forward when tired.

Try “stand a little taller,” without arching your back. If holding the posture becomes hard work, reduce the run’s demand. A torso position does not prove that a muscle has stopped working.

My shoulders keep tightening.

Soften your hands and shoulders. Keep your normal arm height. Check whether the same easy pace feels less tense. Use less of the cue if you have to keep forcing it.

What is cadence?

Cadence is steps per minute, counting both feet. It is a setting, not a score. You do not need to hit 180 simply because someone else does. [3]

When a small increase is useful, keep the speed comparable and check how it feels. Five percent above 160 is 168. That is arithmetic, not your prescription. [1]

Open the practice metronome

One beat is one step. Choose a rate you intend to practice.

Choose 100–220 steps per minute. Plays for up to 60 seconds.

Describe what actually changed.

“My shoulders felt less tense at the same easy speed” is a useful observation. “This fixed my form and made me more efficient” says more than that observation proves.

Try the cue briefly, return to your usual running, then compare again. Video, watch data and what you feel can each help. None is a complete grade of your running.

Do not practice through pain. Stop and get assessment for persistent or recurring pain, especially when it changes your stride. [4]

Use it this week.

On 1 familiar easy run, notice what feels costly. Try 1 cue for 20–30 seconds without speeding up, let it go, then compare. Repeat another day before deciding it helped.

Three mistakes to catch.

  1. Changing several things at once.
  2. Treating a heel strike or loud step as a diagnosis.
  3. Forcing a universal cadence number.

Where does it fit?

Form practice helps you explore a useful change. It sits alongside appropriate running and strength work; appearance alone cannot choose your training.

The part to take with you

Remember this.

  1. Notice a real cost before changing movement.
  2. Try one cue at a familiar easy speed.
  3. Keep only what helps when you check again.
A video shows your foot landing differently from a fast runner’s. Is that enough reason to change it?

No. First ask whether your movement is costing you effort, comfort or control. A picture alone does not tell you what needs fixing.
