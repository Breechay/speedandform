What do these running words mean?

A useful training word should tell you what to do or what to notice. Here are the common words, one plain meaning at a time.

Pace and effort.

Pace: time per mile or kilometer. A smaller time means faster running. Six minutes per kilometer and six minutes per mile are different speeds.

Effort: how demanding the run is for you. The same pace can feel different on a hot day or a hill. Choose your effort →

Easy: an effort that lets you chat comfortably. Zone 2: a label in a zone system. Check how your system defines it before assuming the two mean exactly the same thing.

Threshold and tempo.

Threshold work: strong sustained running you keep under control. Think of turning music up while keeping it clear. Laboratory definitions differ; your breathing is a cue, not an exact test. [1]

Tempo: a broad coaching label. It can mean threshold or a steadier, less demanding effort. Read the effort and duration, not only the name. Learn the threshold lesson →

Session, intervals, repetitions and strides.

Session: one planned piece of training, including preparation and finish.

Intervals: work separated by recovery. Repetition: one piece of that work. In 3 × 6 minutes, you run six minutes three times. The multiplication sign means “repeat,” not “run faster.”

Recovery jog / float: running between the work pieces. The intended effort and time must be stated. A float is not automatically brisk.

Strides: brief, smooth accelerations with recovery. They are not all-out sprints. Compare the session types →

VO₂max: your oxygen-use ceiling.

VO₂max is the highest rate at which your body can take in and use oxygen during hard exercise. Think of it as a ceiling on one part of your aerobic ability.

A high ceiling helps, but does not tell you how fast you will race. How much of that capacity you sustain, how much energy your running costs, and your preparation also matter. [2]

“VO₂max workout” usually names the session’s aim. Completing it does not prove that your VO₂max rose. A watch value is an estimate.

Running economy: the energy cost of a pace.

Think of fuel use in two cars driving at the same speed. Running economy asks how much energy the runner uses at a given speed. [2]

Looking smooth, feeling relaxed and seeing a lower heart rate can be useful observations. They do not directly measure running economy. Your form is how you move; economy is a measured cost.

Lactate: useful fuel, not just waste.

Lactate is made and used by your body during exercise. It can supply energy to other tissues. [3]

A blood lactate number reflects both how much appears and how much is removed. It does not directly tell you the clearance rate. Fatigue has more than one cause.

Cadence and running form.

Cadence: steps per minute, counting both feet. There is no universal target everyone must hit.

Running form: how you move while running. A useful cue changes something that costs you effort, comfort or control. Learn one-change practice →

Fuel and recovery.

Fuel: food and drink that support the work. The amount and timing depend on the session and what you tolerate. It is not a permission signal your body needs before it can run. [4]

Recovery: restoring readiness after work. Sleep, food and manageable training matter. An easy run is not always the best recovery choice. Food and drink → · Run, reduce or rest →

Sleep.

Sleep supports health, recovery and learning. Enough total time matters alongside quality and routine. Individual needs differ; sleep-stage numbers from a watch are estimates. [5] Read the sleep guide →

Consistency and progression.

Consistency: returning to useful work over time, while adjusting when needed. It does not mean never missing a day.

Progression: changing the challenge when your response supports it. More time, speed, days and less rest are different changes. Repeating a useful session can be the right choice.

Training block, cycle and taper.

Block / cycle: a period with a main training purpose. The same workout can play a different role in a different block.

Taper: reducing training before a race so you arrive fresher. It is not a last-minute attempt to catch up on missed fitness. Prepare for race week →

The field and the practice.

On older FORM pages, “the field” names the place where runners train together. For current joining details, use the Thursday page. An old schedule is not a new assignment.

Use it this week.

Take 1 session from your plan and translate it into ordinary words. For example, 3 × 6 minutes means 3 work pieces lasting 6 minutes each. Then find the effort and recovery; the numbers alone are incomplete.

Three mistakes to catch.

  1. Assuming tempo means the same effort for every coach.
  2. Treating a watch estimate as a laboratory measurement.
  3. Confusing pace with effort.

The part to take with you

Remember this.

  1. Pace is a number. Effort is how hard it feels.
  2. Intervals describe the format, not one speed.
  3. A measurement and a feeling are different kinds of evidence.
Your watch says your running economy improved because your heart rate was lower. What can you say for sure?

You can say the watch recorded a lower heart rate. That alone is not a direct measurement of the energy needed to run at that speed. Conditions and other factors matter too.
