The deal
The problemYou can run fast when you're fresh. Late in a long run, the same pace can disappear.

The goalArrive late in the marathon with useful pace still available — not just a bigger pile of tired miles.
01 · Who it's for

YOU SHOULD ALREADY KNOW WHAT A BIG WEEK FEELS LIKE.

This is not where you learn to run six days, build your first long run or guess a goal pace. You should already be doing those things consistently.

This is probably you

  • Your 5K, 10K or half-marathon fitness looks better than your marathon finish.
  • ~50 miles per week is already normal for you.
  • Six running days and 16–18 mile long runs are familiar.
  • You have a pace supported by recent racing or workouts — not just a goal you picked.
  • You want to keep some speed in the week while getting much better late.

Start somewhere else if

  • This is your first marathon build.
  • You are returning from injury or rebuilding basic mileage.
  • 50–66 miles per week would be a major jump.
  • Your target pace only exists because of a finish-time goal.
  • You need weekly individualized changes from a coach.
02 · How it works

THE PACE STAYS.
WE MOVE IT LATER.

Think of the key pace like a marker on a road. Early in the plan you reach it while you are fresh. Later, we keep moving that same marker farther down the road.

The pace is not the progression. The depth is.

A common response to late fading

Just add more miles.

More endurance can help. But if the only answer is more easy volume, you may never practice finding useful pace when the run is already expensive.

Saturday · Ask

Move the same tool later.

The hard part is not making the pace faster. It is asking for useful pace after more running has already happened.

Fueling is part of the workoutIf the fuel changes, the test changes.

From the first marathon-specific long run, practice the strategy you intend to race with and record what you actually took. A late fade means very little if the fueling was improvised.

Long-run rule

DON'T TURN TRAINING INTO THE RACE.

The point is to create enough prior running that the late pace means something — without needing to run the whole marathon in training. More miles are not automatically better. Race day is the full test.

Specific reserve

The reserve is the faster capability supported by current evidence. It is not automatically marathon race-day pace. The exact band is athlete-relative and can change when race or training evidence changes the coach read.

Marathon rhythm

Marathon rhythm is what can actually be distributed across 26.2. It appears deliberately after the reserve has been protected; it does not replace the ceiling or the faster capability above race demand.

04 · Coming from a race

YOU JUST RACED.
DON'T START ANOTHER TEST YET.

Take two weeks to let the last block land. Week 1 is recovery. Week 2 is a return to normal running. No proving, no catching up, no apology for being tired.
Transition 01

Recover

4–5 easy running days · roughly 24–30 miles or less · no pace demand · no required long-run achievement.

Transition 02

Return

5–6 running days · roughly 34–40 miles · easy running · relaxed 9–10 mile long run · one small strides or hill touch if comfortable.

05 · Coach FAQ

THREE THINGS FAST RUNNERS
ASK FIRST.

The simple answers matter more than extra theory.
Won't all the easy running make me slow?

No. Easy running builds the endurance underneath the week; the faster work never disappears. The goal is not to replace speed with mileage. It is to make the speed easier to carry.

Why isn't the mileage even higher?

More mileage can help if you can absorb it. The plan uses enough volume to build the marathon without treating a bigger number as the goal. The key question is what you can still do late, not who logged the most miles.

What if I feel amazing early on race day?

Good. Keep it that way. The marathon rewards patience. Feeling easy at mile 10 is not permission to spend what you will need at mile 22.
