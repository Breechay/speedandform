# FORM — The Athletic Life

Independent strategy report for Brice Ikouebe · Speed & Form / FORM · October 28, 2026

Exploratory research. Not authorization to build, publish, contact athletes, change prescriptions or modify production systems.

## Read this first: what this report stands on

This report works only from the brief. I had no repository access, no private data and no athlete conversations, so nothing here is observed behavior. Evidence is tagged throughout:

- [Ops] a fact or count stated in the brief about FORM’s actual operations.

- [Published] general findings from the research literature, recalled from background knowledge. I tried to open the brief’s six starting-point links and the fetch was rate-limited, so I have not read those papers and rely on none of their specifics. Verify before citing.

- [Inference] a reasoned conclusion from the above.

- [Hypothesis] an idea with no evidence yet. Most of the opportunities below are this.

No roster, demographics, usage figures or athlete preferences have been invented.

## A. Independent thesis

FORM’s rarest asset is not the plan, the app or the community. It is a coach who knows each person well enough to be right about them when they are least sure of themselves.

Those moments are mostly not training days. They are the days around deciding to enter a race, the week after a goal race, the first week back from an injury, the quiet stretch when life gets busy, the evening before a start line in an unfamiliar city. The prescription handles ordinary days well. Almost nothing currently handles the transitions, and transitions are where people drift, doubt or quietly stop. [Inference; to be tested]

The brief’s own numbers point at one such transition. [Ops] Of 26 race entries, 7 are marked registered and 19 are target or alternative. That may simply be a normal planning horizon. It may also be a gap between wanting to race somewhere and actually going, which is exactly the gap where an informed, trusted human adds value and a generic platform cannot.

My thesis: FORM could become the steward of an athlete’s whole athletic year: a person-led service, with software as its ledger, that attends to transitions and turns ambitions into attempts. Strava, Garmin and Nike cannot copy it because it depends on Brice’s judgment about specific people, a small room of familiar faces and a physical place to gather.

### Where I would push back on the brief

- Segmenting by runner type is the wrong cut. Coached, competitive, developing, hybrid, independent, social and remote describe who someone is. The more useful cut is where someone is in their season (choosing, building, tapering, racing, recovering, returning, dormant) crossed with how they relate to FORM (coached, independent, social). A person’s needs change far more between “week after the race” and “week four of a block” than between “competitive” and “developing”.

- The brief’s three illustrative ideas are all about connection. I would test the quieter ones first: decision support, debriefs and Brice’s own attention. They are cheaper and use assets you already have.

- Racecation is a downstream product. It only makes sense if athletes overlap at races. [Ops] 26 entries across 13 events is about 2 per event on average. The distribution (how many events have 3 or more athletes) is unknown and decides whether any shared-event idea is viable.

### Three meaningfully different futures

Future 1: The Steward (person-led, built around transitions). FORM owns the seams between training blocks: race decisions, race week, post-race debriefs, setbacks and returns, and a private record of each athlete’s real ambitions. The app is a ledger and a coach-side aid. The athlete mostly experiences a person who always seems to be a step ahead. Requires: Brice’s judgment protected by good coach-side tooling, plus a part-time operations helper. Fails if: the transitions are already handled informally, or Brice’s time has no room for them.

Future 2: The Third Place (a place and a rhythm). FORM is a physical practice: Thursday track, a recurring morning, a place to arrive and a standing welcome. Hospitality is the core competence. The app is a doorway and a schedule. Requires: reliable local attendance, a venue and Brice’s presence. Fails if: too many athletes are remote, busy or independent to benefit; it also serves Miami far better than everyone else.

Future 3: The Living Record (storyteller and keeper of identity). FORM is where an athlete’s year is witnessed: portraits, race films, an annual book, a coach’s account of what changed. Belonging travels by artifact, so remote athletes belong as much as locals. Requires: sustained photographic and editorial production. Fails if: it is delightful but changes no behavior, and consumes the founder’s limited creative hours.

Considered and set aside: a social network or athlete-matching platform. With roughly three dozen athletes and thin race overlap, a network has too few edges to be useful, and it moves the center of gravity from Brice’s judgment to an algorithm.

Favored: Future 1, delivered through Future 2 and compounding into Future 3. The Steward fits the evidence best: it addresses the one visible gap in the operational numbers, it works for remote and independent people, it is the cheapest to test, and it scales with a coach’s judgment rather than with engineering. The Third Place is the best place to deliver it, and the Living Record is what the stewardship leaves behind.

Assumptions this requires:

- Unmet need concentrates at transitions, not in the training weeks themselves.

- The target-to-registered gap reflects missing decision support, not just ordinary uncertainty, money or timing.

- Enough athletes want FORM to be present in their lives beyond a prescription.

- Brice can afford the minutes, especially if software removes his administrative load.

What could make it wrong: the value athletes actually get is the narrowness of a high-trust coach relationship, and extending it feels intrusive or dilutes it. That risk is real and is the largest unresolved question (section J).

## B. Needs and opportunity map

So about 11 records have no training block, and about 19 have no race entry. Who they are is unknown, and they may be the most informative interviews in this whole program. These are database counts, not engagement, attendance or revenue.

1. Turning a target into a decision. Who: athletes with open target races. Evidence: [Ops] 19 target/alternative vs 7 registered; interpretation unknown. [Published] planning and specific implementation intentions narrow the intention–behavior gap, with modest effects. Status: [Inference]. Value: high if the gap is real.

2. Support at transitions: after a race, after a setback, after a break. Who: everyone eventually, first the athletes finishing goal races. Evidence: none in FORM’s data yet. [Published, general] adherence research and sport psychology describe loss of structure and identity after goal events and during injury. Status: [Hypothesis]. Value: potentially the highest.

3. Being known between sessions. Who: committed athletes, remote athletes. Evidence: [Ops] concierge “B.” acknowledgements exist; their effect has not been measured. [Published] relatedness is associated with autonomous motivation. Status: [Hypothesis].

4. Understanding why, not just what. Who: developing and returning athletes especially. Evidence: [Published] informative feedback supports perceived competence and self-efficacy. Status: [Hypothesis]. Value: medium; cheap to test.

5. Company for those who train alone, or away. Who: remote athletes, solo trainers. Evidence: [Published] research on running partners links partner support to continued running (one of the brief’s starting points; not read here). Status: [Hypothesis] for this roster.

6. Brice’s own cognitive load. Who: Brice, and every athlete indirectly. Evidence: [Ops] 34 linked athletes and 24 active blocks, plus messages, races and personal context. Status: [Inference]: a person cannot hold that many evolving relationships in his head without something that surfaces what changed.

7. Belonging without a plan. Who: the roughly 11 records with no training block, independents and social athletes. Evidence: none. Status: unknown. This is the largest blind spot.

9. Logistics for doing the things that matter. Who: busy athletes, anyone traveling to race. Evidence: none directly beyond the brief’s framing. Status: [Hypothesis].

## C. The psychology of the FORM experience

Unless noted, these are [Published, general] and associational. Treat them as reasons to run small tests, not proof that a feature works.

- Autonomy, competence, relatedness (self-determination theory). Associated with autonomous motivation and persistence in physical activity. Much of the evidence is cross-sectional. Legitimate lever: explain the reasoning behind a session and keep decisions with the athlete. Harm: “support” that becomes controlling erodes autonomy. A coach who decides everything about someone’s season, including races, can do this.

- Self-efficacy from mastery. The strongest source of confidence is doing something hard and interpreting it correctly. Lever: debriefs that help someone see what they accomplished. Harm: interpretation that misses, or over-praise that rings false.

- Intention–behavior gap and planning. Specific plans help modestly; effects shrink in stricter trials. Lever: turn “I’d like to race X” into a dated decision with logistics. Harm: manufactured urgency or deadlines that feel like pressure.

- Social support and group exercise. Cohesion and company are associated with adherence, but people who join groups are already motivated, so causality is uncertain. Harm: attendance pressure, in-groups and out-groups inside a roster of 36.

- Friendship formation. Repeated shared time matters and takes more of it than most expect. One widely cited study reported dozens of hours to move from acquaintance to casual friend. [Published; verify] An introduction alone will not make a friendship. It needs a reason and a first shared activity.

- Social comparison and aspiration. Seeing someone similar succeed can inspire; seeing someone far ahead often demoralizes, especially when the gap feels unattainable. Harm: visible performance and race ambitions of fast athletes shown to developing ones. Race visibility should be opt-in and carry no performance numbers.

- Recognition and memory. A specific, true observation from a person who knows you reads as care; a template reads as marketing. This is an [Inference] consistent with the brief’s own rule that automation must never masquerade as Brice’s attention.

- Cost and equity. Travel and race fees filter participation by money. Anything built on trips quietly privileges those who can afford them.

## D. Thirteen opportunities

Ordered by my confidence in value per unit of Brice’s time. Items 1–6 follow here; 7–13 follow in the next part.

### 1. Post-race debrief and next chapter

- Need: After a goal race, structure and meaning vanish when motivation is least anchored, and nobody helps interpret what happened.

- First beneficiary: athletes finishing an A-race.

- In real life: within about 72 hours Brice sends a voice note or calls: what did you notice, what surprised you, what do you want next. Within a week, one paragraph on what he saw and a proposed next chapter, which may be rest, a short race, a new focus or nothing.

- Assets: coaching relationship, race records, training record.

- Why it may work: mastery is interpreted, not just completed. [Inference] Post-event lulls are a plausible drop-off point; unverified for this roster.

- Minimum version: the next three finishers; a 10-minute call; notes kept privately.

- App needed: no. Later, a prompt visible only to Brice.

- Burden on Brice: about 15–20 minutes per race, bounded by races rather than days.

- Privacy: notes stay private; nothing shared without the athlete’s approval.

- Test: does the athlete name a next step they did not have? Would they ask for it back after three races?

- Confidence: medium-high on value, low on evidence. [Hypothesis]

### 2. Race decision desk

- Need: turn “I should do that race” into a yes, a no or an alternative, with a plan.

- First beneficiary: athletes with target races in roughly the next four to six months.

- In real life: for each target Brice gives a short verdict (go, not this one, consider that instead) with a reason tied to the athlete’s block, plus a one-page practicalities sheet: registration window, course character, travel, weather. The athlete decides.

- Assets: race records, blocks, coaching judgment.

- Why it may work: moves intention to a specific plan while keeping the decision with the athlete. [Published, modest effects]

- Minimum version: audit the 19 target or alternative entries; talk through five.

- App needed: no. Possibly clearer race statuses later.

- Burden: moderate up front; a helper can prepare the practicalities sheets.

- Privacy: nothing shown to other athletes.

- Test: share of targets that become a decision within two weeks. Count “no” as a success; saved effort reported by athletes.

- Confidence: medium. [Inference]

### 3. The shared-event table

- Need: companionship and low-friction adventure around a race people are already going to.

- First beneficiary: athletes registered for the same event who opt in.

- In real life: when three or more opted-in athletes are at one event, Brice or a designated host arranges a shakeout meeting point, a dinner and a one-page logistics note. No bus, no package, no sales.

- Assets: Race Calendar, trust, hosting skill.

- Why it may work: shared experience supports relatedness, and companions support continued running. [Published, associational]

- Minimum version: one event, one message to opted-in athletes.

- App needed: no; at most a per-race consent control.

- Burden: low if local; travel is the constraint.

- Privacy: opt-in per race, never per profile; no home location; no automatic exposure of travel plans.

- Test: did people attend who otherwise would not have? Did any feel pressured?

- Confidence: low-medium. Overlap density is unverified.

### 4. Brice’s Monday attention list

- Need: one person cannot hold dozens of evolving relationships in memory.

- First beneficiary: Brice; athletes benefit indirectly.

- In real life: a one-page weekly list for Brice only: who has gone quiet, who has a race within 14 days, who changed a plan, who raised a concern, a milestone, an unanswered message, whom he has not spoken to in several weeks. He decides what to do. Nothing is sent automatically.

- Assets: filings, blocks, messages, race entries, coach surfaces.

- Why it may work: it is a retrieval problem, not a relationship problem. [Inference]

- Minimum version: a weekly query output as a text page.

- App needed: small, coach-side.

- Burden: negative if kept to one page.

- Privacy: coach-only; health notes excluded unless Brice adds them.

- Test: does Brice act on three or more items a week? Does it catch things he would have missed?

- Confidence: medium-high. [Inference]

### 5. Session marginalia

- Need: understanding why, not just what.

- First beneficiary: developing and returning athletes.

- In real life: on one or two key sessions per block, a two-sentence note written or voiced by Brice: “This is the session that tells me whether…”.

- Assets: coaching judgment, the Week surface if it supports notes. [Unverified]

- Why it may work: informative feedback supports perceived competence. [Published, general]

- Minimum version: five athletes, one block.

- App needed: no, if a message will do.

- Burden: two to three minutes each.

- Privacy: private. It must be authored by Brice. Generated notes presented as his would break a stated rule.

- Test: do athletes quote it back? Do “why” questions drop? Would they notice if it stopped?

- Confidence: medium. [Hypothesis]

### 6. The Thursday arrival card

- Need: to feel expected; individual prescriptions inside a shared session.

- First beneficiary: regular track attendees, newcomers and visitors.

- In real life: each athlete finds a printed card with their name, today’s session in their prescription and one line from Brice. Newcomers are introduced to one person. Afterwards, a few unhurried minutes and something to drink.

- Assets: prescription data, hospitality instinct, the standing Thursday practice.

- Why it may work: it makes individual attention visible and reduces sheet-checking and sideways comparison. [Inference]

- Minimum version: print cards for the next four Thursdays.

- App needed: no; printed from existing data.

- Burden: low-moderate; a helper can own it.

- Privacy: each card shows only that athlete’s work.

- Test: fewer logistics questions, time to first rep, whether athletes keep the card.

- Confidence: medium. [Hypothesis]

### 7. Race-week page

- Need: certainty and calm in the final days before a start line, especially in an unfamiliar place.

- First beneficiary: athletes with a registered race.

- In real life: about five days out, one page: course character, weather, pacing intentions consistent with the prescription, logistics, and one line Brice wrote himself. On race morning, a short message from him.

- Assets: race records, blocks, coaching judgment, the existing personal acknowledgement style.

- Why it may work: it lowers cognitive load when arousal is high and builds self-efficacy through preparation. [Inference]

- Minimum version: the next three races; a template in a document; a helper fills logistics, Brice writes the line.

- App needed: no. It could later live beside the Race Calendar entry.

- Burden: about ten minutes of Brice per race.

- Privacy: pace plans stay private and never appear on a shared calendar.

- Test: fewer last-week preparation questions; athletes report feeling settled; how many actually open it.

- Confidence: medium-high. [Hypothesis]

### 8. Brice’s introductions

- Need: useful friendships and training company, such as someone at a similar pace in a new city or someone facing the same race.

- First beneficiary: athletes who train alone or are new to the group.

- In real life: Brice keeps a private introduction ledger and makes at most one a month, by double opt-in. Each introduction is a message from him saying why, with a suggested first shared thing, such as coffee after Thursday.

- Assets: his knowledge of people, and the trust attached to his name.

- Why it may work: friendship needs repeated shared time, so an introduction needs a reason and a first activity. [Published; verify]

- Minimum version: three introductions in six weeks, no tooling.

- App needed: no. Deliberately not.

- Burden: low in minutes, high in judgment; it must stay with Brice.

- Privacy: names, locations and schedules are exchanged only after both agree.

- Test: did they meet, did they meet a second time without Brice, did anyone feel awkward?

- Confidence: low-medium. Strong logic, little evidence. [Hypothesis]

### 9. Setback and return pathway

- Need: injured, ill or returning athletes lose training, identity and the group at once, and often need a competent referral.

- First beneficiary: anyone currently unable to train normally.

- In real life: a check-in from Brice within two days; a warm introduction to a trusted physio or sports-medicine partner, with only what the athlete approves; a role in the group (timekeeper, host, photographer) so they stay present; a planned return conversation.

- Assets: coaching relationship, a short list of trusted specialists, Thursday practice.

- Why it may work: loss of athletic identity during injury is widely described in sport psychology, and staying connected is thought to help adjustment. [Published, general; hedge]

- Minimum version: shortlist two specialists, write one check-in script, offer one role.

- App needed: no.

- Burden: occasional and high-value when it happens.

- Privacy: health information is sensitive. Brice does not diagnose. The athlete controls every disclosure.

- Test: time to return to consistent training, retention, and whether offered roles felt patronizing.

- Confidence: medium. [Inference]

### 10. Aspiration ledger

- Need: ambitions that are never said aloud (a first marathon abroad, a comeback, a trip, a time) rarely get attempted.

- First beneficiary: every athlete, and Brice’s ability to serve them.

- In real life: one conversation in which each athlete names one to three things they would like to have done in a year, in their own words. Brice holds them privately and revisits quarterly: is this still true, and what is in the way? The ledger feeds the decision desk, introductions and events.

- Assets: the coaching relationship; any private coach-note field.

- Why it may work: goals that are self-endorsed are associated with better persistence than goals imposed from outside. [Published, general]

- Minimum version: add one question to each athlete’s next check-in.

- App needed: no; a private note is enough.

- Burden: low.

- Privacy: private by default; shared only with explicit consent.

- Test: how many ambitions does Brice learn that he did not know, and how many become a decision within a quarter.

- Confidence: medium-high. [Inference]

### 11. The independent and social lane

- Need: some people do not want a plan from FORM but value the people, events and standards; others already have a coach and platform.

- First beneficiary: [Ops] the roughly 11 records with no training block, plus anyone with another coach.

- In real life: an explicit way to belong without filing anything: invitation to open mornings and events, optional race visibility, and Brice remembering their goals without prescribing. FORM stays useful without installation.

- Assets: culture, hosted practice, the principle that the network must not depend on the app.

- Why it may work: relatedness can come from belonging, not from being coached. [Hypothesis]

- Minimum version: talk to three or four of these people before designing anything.

- App needed: probably not.

- Burden: low if defined; diffuse if not.

- Privacy: no implied coach authority; consent for anything shown.

- Test: do they show up, and do they say it is for them?

- Confidence: low. We do not know who these people are.

### 12. The Bench Exchange (unconventional)

- Need: contribution and reciprocity. Athletes probably have expertise in many fields, and being useful is a form of belonging.

- First beneficiary: athletes who like to teach or be taught.

- In real life: a small occasional evening where one athlete teaches twenty minutes on something they know, not running, over food. Entirely voluntary.

- Assets: a trusted room, hospitality.

- Why it may work: reciprocity builds social capital and recognition. [Hypothesis]

- Minimum version: ask in interviews, “What do you know well that people here might find useful?” Run one only if at least three say yes.

- App needed: no.

- Burden: low-medium.

- Privacy: no one is pressed to disclose a profession; no obligation to attend or give.

- Test: attendance, repeat requests, any hint of obligation.

- Confidence: low. Pure hypothesis.

### 13. The season book

- Need: recognition; the sense that a year happened and someone saw it.

- First beneficiary: athletes with a meaningful season.

- In real life: a small printed piece at season end: a race portrait, a few honest numbers, Brice’s note on what changed, and nothing manufactured.

- Assets: photography and film, training and race records, editorial taste.

- Why it may work: narrative identity and a tangible artifact. [Hypothesis; published support is thin]

- Minimum version: one folded sheet for three athletes.

- App needed: no; an export can come later.

- Burden: high in curation and photography; a helper can handle production.

- Privacy: only what the athlete approves; nothing medical.

- Test: is it displayed or shared unprompted? Would they want another?

- Confidence: medium on delight, low on any behavior change.

## E. Three exceptional moments

1. The card at the track (physical and human). An athlete arrives on a Thursday. A card at the edge of the track has their name and their session in their paces, with one line from Brice that could only be about them: “Don’t chase the first two. The last one is where it counts.” A newcomer finds she is introduced to one person, by name, with a reason. Nothing is announced. Afterwards, a few unhurried minutes and a drink, and nobody is rushed out. The thought is not “nice event” but “he knew I was coming.”

2. The page five days out. An athlete traveling to a race opens a single page: the course, the weather, where the start actually is, a pacing intention that matches what they have trained, and a line from Brice in his own voice about this athlete’s particular tendency to go out too fast. On race morning there is a short message and nothing else. The thought is: “He has already thought about my race.”

3. The call after. Three days after a race, Brice calls. He does not open with the time. He asks what the athlete noticed first. A week later a short note arrives proposing the next chapter, and it already contains what they said on the phone. The thought is: “He was listening, and he remembered.”

## F. What to improve immediately

Do first (next two weeks)

- Make the race record trustworthy. Audit the 19 target or alternative entries: which are live, passed or abandoned? Check whether the data separates considering, decided, registered, completed and not pursued, and whether there is a decide-by date. Produce a one-off, anonymized count of how many athletes are at each event. This single query tells you whether shared-event ideas are viable and whether the decision-desk hypothesis is real.

- Pull baseline behaviors from existing records (see G), before changing anything, so you can tell later whether anything changed.

- Run the post-race debrief and race-week page by hand for the next few races.

- Start the Monday attention list as a plain query and text page.

- Print Thursday cards for four weeks.

Do next (this quarter)

- Interview the athletes with no block and the least-engaged athletes first (see G).

- Define who covers when Brice is away, so continuity does not rest on one person.

- Confirm the race-visibility defaults. If any athlete’s race entries are visible to others without an explicit choice, change that before adding anything social.

Wait

- Any new notification type. The brief notes delivery still requires real-device validation, so no service step should depend on it yet.

- Public performance numbers, feeds, streaks and attendance displays.

- Anything that asks athletes for more reporting or decisions.

## G. How to validate this with athletes

With roughly 35 active records there is no statistics to run. The aim is to understand specific people well, watch what they actually do, and cheaply remove anything that does not earn its place.

Ground rules

- Tell athletes plainly that this is learning, nothing is being sold, and declining costs nothing.

- Brice is a conflicted interviewer. People tell their coach what he hopes to hear. Have a neutral person run a meaningful share of conversations, or collect voice or written answers privately.

- Use only authorized, minimal, aggregated data. No individual dossiers or health details in any write-up.

Phase 0: baseline from what already exists (weeks 1–2). Before changing anything, record aggregate figures from existing records: how long target races sit before being registered or dropped; how many athletes are at each event; sessions filed in the two weeks after a race versus the two weeks before; gaps between blocks; Thursday attendance; typical message response times. This reveals actual behavior at the transitions the thesis depends on.

Phase 1: ten to twelve interviews (weeks 2–5), thirty to forty minutes each. Sample across situations, not personas: two or three committed coached athletes; two competitive; two developing or returning; one or two hybrid; two or three from the no-block, independent or social group; two remote; and two who have gone quiet. The quiet ones tell you the most.

Ask about the past, not the hypothetical:

- Tell me about the last time you decided to enter a race. Who was involved, and what almost stopped you?

- What did the 48 hours before your last start line feel like? What would have helped?

- What did you do the week after your last race? How did you feel about training then?

- Tell me about a time you nearly stopped training. What happened?

- Who do you run with outside FORM? How did that start?

- What have you wanted to do in the last year that you did not? What got in the way?

- What does FORM give you that you could not easily get elsewhere?

- What do you know well, outside running, that others here might find useful?

Do not ask: “Would you use a racecation feature?”, “Would you like to see who else is racing?”, “Would more social features help?”, or anything that names our existing ideas. People will say yes to be agreeable.

Phase 2: five small experiments (weeks 4–12). Each runs by hand, on a few people, with a stop condition.

- Post-race debrief for the next 3–6 finishers. Stop if: athletes treat it as an obligation or answers get shorter.

- Decision desk for five athletes with open targets. Stop if: no decision changes, or athletes report it as pressure.

- Thursday arrival cards for four sessions. Stop if: athletes ignore them, or Brice’s setup time exceeds the benefit.

- One shared-event table, only if Phase 0 finds an event with three or more opted-in athletes. Stop if: fewer than two attend or anyone feels pressed.

- Aspiration question asked of everyone at their next check-in. Stop if: it reveals nothing Brice did not already know.

What counts as evidence: decisions made, races selected or dropped, introductions that led to a second meeting, hosted events attended, minutes of Brice’s time per ritual (log them), and athletes saying in their own words that something was clearer or lighter. What does not count: app opens, taps, streak lengths or anything measuring attention rather than benefit.

Detecting that an idea is unwanted or intrusive. Replies get shorter or slower; people decline politely twice; someone asks you to stop; attendance drops after a hosted event; athletes need an explanation of what the ritual is for; or Brice’s minutes per athlete exceed the value created. The cleanest check is a withdrawal test: after six weeks, quietly pause a ritual and see who asks for it back. If nobody does, it was decoration.

## H. A practical operating model

What Brice owns, personally and non-delegably

- Prescription and all coaching judgment.

- The words that carry his voice: debrief calls, race-week lines, introductions, setback check-ins and aspiration conversations.

- Who is in the room and who meets whom.

- Any decision involving an athlete’s health, goals or privacy.

What a helper or partner can own

- Race research: registration windows, travel, weather and course notes (the practicalities sheet).

- Printing and prepping Thursday cards, scheduling and hosting logistics.

- Photo selection and production for any season book.

- A short list of trusted specialists (physio, sports medicine, nutrition), each with a clean, athlete-approved handoff.

- A part-time operations helper, even a few hours a week, is likely worth more than additional app engineering. [Inference]

What software or AI may do safely

- Draft practicalities sheets and logistics for Brice or the helper to review.

- Build the Monday attention list from existing records, visible only to Brice.

- Track deadlines and remind Brice, not athletes.

- Transcribe his voice notes and file them.

What software or AI must not do

- Send anything that appears to be Brice’s personal observation or attention.

- Alter or recommend changes to prescriptions autonomously.

- Decide introductions, share athlete data or expose schedules, locations or goals.

Keeping intimacy as the roster grows Treat Brice’s attention as the constrained resource and protect it. Keep hosted groups small, add coaches or hosts who are trained in the same standard rather than growing one central feed, and cap the number of athletes each person serves at the level where the transition rituals still happen on time. I have no evidence for what that number is; log the minutes for six weeks and set it from reality. Judgment-heavy work (the call after a race) stays human. Retrieval and logistics move to software and a helper.

## I. What not to build

- An athlete-matching algorithm or social graph. Opportunity cost: engineering time and a drift from personal curation toward automation, on a roster too small to supply the data. Better: Brice’s introduction ledger.

- Feeds, kudos, leaderboards, streaks and attendance displays. Opportunity cost: they create comparison and obligation, which the brief already rejects. Better: the personal acknowledgements that exist, and rare, chosen recognition.

- More analytics and AI insight dashboards. Opportunity cost: athletes already have these from other platforms, and numbers rarely create understanding. Better: two hand-written sentences on the sessions that matter.

- Automated “personal” messages or AI check-ins in Brice’s voice. Opportunity cost: one discovered fake erodes the trust the whole service depends on. Better: an attention list that helps Brice write the real message faster.

- A racecation or retreat product with payments, packages and liability, built before demand is shown. Opportunity cost: operational, legal and financial weight, plus an equity problem when trips filter by income. Better: a shared-event table at races people have already chosen. Revisit only if repeated overlap and clear demand appear.

## J. Final recommendation

1. The strongest strategic direction. Make FORM the steward of the athletic year: a person-led service that attends to the transitions between training (choosing a race, race week, the week after, a setback, a return) and keeps a private ledger of each athlete’s real ambitions. Deliver it through a hosted practice and compound it into a record of the athlete’s story. Do not make it a social platform; if that conclusion is ever earned, the evidence will come from the interviews and the overlap data, not from enthusiasm.

2. Three highest-value actions for existing athletes.

- Audit the race record and start the decision desk.

- Make the post-race debrief and race-week page part of how you coach.

- Give every Thursday athlete a card that shows someone expected them.

The Monday attention list is the enabler that makes all three affordable.

3. One compelling, unconventional experiment: Target Night. Host one evening at a hospitality venue. Each attending athlete brings one race they are considering, on paper. The room, with Brice present, looks at them together. Nobody is asked to commit. Afterwards, check which targets became decisions and whether anyone chose a race because of who else was there. It tests the central hypothesis (shared context converts wanting into doing) with no software and a very small risk, and it needs a human host, which is precisely the kind of thing a generic platform cannot do.

4. The largest unresolved question. Do athletes want FORM to be more present in their lives beyond training, or is the value they get specifically the narrow, high-trust coach relationship that expansion would dilute? Everything above assumes they want a more attentive version of the same relationship, not a larger one.

5. The most important evidence to gather before committing resources.

- The race-entry timeline and event overlap counts.

- Ten to twelve behavior-based interviews, led partly by someone other than Brice, including the no-block and quiet athletes.

- Six weeks of logged minutes for the debrief, race-week page and attention list.

- Who the roughly 11 records with no training block actually are.
