# FORM · The Athletic Life

Independent strategy, human experience and opportunity report  
Prepared for Brice Ikouebe · October 28, 2026

**Recommendation:** Develop FORM as a personal athletic service that preserves continuity across training, competition and changes in life. Make the ordinary week dependable, help athletes understand their development, and selectively make experiences possible that they could not easily arrange alone.

The distinctive asset is Brice's informed judgment about particular people, exercised through a real practice. Software should make that judgment easier to apply and its consequences more dependable. Hospitality should make participation easier. Community should remain something people can choose, including choosing very little of it.

## Scope and evidence discipline

**What is known:** The brief reports 36 athlete records, 35 active records, 34 active coach-account membership links, 25 athletes with training blocks and 24 active blocks. It reports 17 athletes associated with 26 race entries across 13 events: seven entries marked registered and 19 target or alternative. These are supplied database aggregates, not independently reverified here. They establish a small, heterogeneous service population; they do not establish demand, payment, attendance or engagement.

**What remains unverified:** Installed app behavior, physical-device delivery, actual athlete use, willingness to pay, relationship quality, available staff, founder capacity, race travel commitments and the prevalence of each unmet need. Screenshots were not supplied. This is a targeted documentary review, not a full code, security or release audit.

Throughout this report:

## A. Independent thesis: three genuinely different futures

### 1. Personal athletic service

FORM helps a person maintain a coherent athletic life: clear current work, understandable progress, thoughtful transitions and selected practical assistance. Its basic unit is a person over a season. Coaching clients receive prescription and interpretation; independent members can receive hosting or practical help without surrendering their coach relationship.

Its distinguishing outcome is continuity: fewer unresolved questions, less repeated explanation, and appropriate support when circumstances change. The app holds reliable memory and work; Brice supplies judgment. This direction works for local and remote athletes.

**Required assumptions:** People experience consequential uncertainty or coordination friction; Brice can bound the promise; enough work can be handled without him; athletes want this form of attention. **Failure case:** Athletes primarily want precise plans and already manage everything else satisfactorily. In that case, excellent coaching with simpler software is the better business.

### 2. Hosted athletic club

FORM becomes a dependable set of places and occasions: Thursday practice, a recurring table, selected races and occasional excursions. Its basic unit is a hosted gathering. The central investment is reliable hosts, venues and execution, with technology providing logistics and access.

Its distinctive outcome is participation that would otherwise have been difficult: arriving comfortably, knowing someone, sharing a good morning and gradually developing relationships. Independent and community-oriented members benefit without needing training software.

**Required assumptions:** Enough local people repeatedly choose the same times and formats; hosts besides Brice can carry the standard; recurring attendance supports costs. **Failure case:** The apparent community is mostly affection for Brice, while schedules, geography and individual preferences prevent repeated participation. A permanent clubhouse would magnify that risk.

### 3. Athletic development studio

FORM helps people become more capable through coached projects, practical learning, expert access and a credible record of development. Its basic unit is a bounded project: learning race execution, preparing for a new kind of event, building confidence in a skill, or producing a personally meaningful season record.

This future emphasizes teaching and opportunity over routine administration. Competitive athletes might value specific expertise or access; developing athletes might value understanding and mastery; remote athletes could participate through individual reviews and small clinics.

**Required assumptions:** Athletes want deeper learning or new opportunities beyond their plans; Brice can teach without turning every interaction into a seminar; qualified partners add value. **Failure case:** More educational content becomes homework, or the studio's ambitions exceed what athletes actually want from FORM.

### Preferred direction

Choose **the personal athletic service as the core**, test a modest hosted practice around it, and borrow bounded development projects from the studio. Do not launch all three as equal programs. First establish which problems recur and what each person can reasonably count on.

This choice fits the existing investment in individualized training and private records, serves remote members, and does not require a lease or a dense social network. Its biggest danger is unlimited concierge scope. The service should concern athletic decisions, access and experiences, not general life management.

What is difficult for a generic platform to reproduce is the accumulated context behind a good decision: which explanation helps this athlete, which invitation fits their current life, who can host them well, and when leaving them alone is the right service. This is a strategic inference about FORM's assets, not a claim that competitors lack particular features.

**A challenge to the founder's vision:** Feeling known is only valuable when the attention is wanted and useful. The standard should not be “How much care did we display?” It should be “What became easier, clearer or possible for this person?” Beautiful recognition cannot compensate for a lost filing or an unanswered practical question.

### Replace fixed personas with current circumstances

“Competitive,” “social” and “busy” describe overlapping aspects of people. For service decisions, use five temporary questions: What are they trying to do now? What is obstructing it? Who owns their training? What contact or company do they currently want? What decision or transition is approaching?

An experienced athlete can need reassurance after a setback. A socially confident athlete can want solitude during a demanding work period. A non-app member can be central to a gathering. Do not convert these circumstances into permanent labels or inferred psychological profiles. Record only what helps deliver an agreed service, preferably in the athlete's own words.

## C. Psychology: plausible mechanisms and their limits

### Evidence assessment

| Source | What the evidence supports | Limits for FORM |
|---|---|---|
| R1: Xu et al., 2025, review of seven reviews | Competence and autonomous forms of motivation are relevant to physical activity | An umbrella review is not seven independent experimental replications. It cannot validate a FORM feature or prove that recognition causes adherence |
| R2: Franken et al., 2022, three-wave panel of more than 800 race registrants | Changes in co-runners were associated with running frequency; partner characteristics were associated with showing up for the race | Longitudinal association, not randomized assignment. Selection and changing circumstances remain possible explanations |
| R3: Yang et al., 2025, 92 studies, 47,548 participants | Intention and behavior were moderately correlated, r=.41; planning and action control featured in statistical pathways | Meta-analytic mediation does not establish a causal sequence. It does not show that reminders are the missing intervention |
| R4: Pereira et al., 2021, 58 studies | Recreational running literature covers motives, self-regulation and psychological outcomes | Thirty-one studies were rated poor quality, 20 fair and seven good; methods varied substantially. Generalization to serious competitive training is limited |
| R5: Gao et al., 2026, 21 group-fitness studies | Behavior-specific self-efficacy had more consistent support than broad social-support measures | Predominantly observational, heterogeneous and focused on instructor-led classes, not individualized endurance coaching |
| R6: Cui et al., 2026, 16 amateur-marathon studies | Motives are multidimensional; health, achievement, psychological and social dimensions differ by context | Mostly European evidence, moderate overall study quality. Claims of changing motives are not necessarily within-person longitudinal findings |
| R7: Hall, 2019, two studies | Shared time, leisure activities and everyday talk were associated with increasing closeness | Relocating adults and first-year students are imperfect analogues. No friendship-hours quota should be turned into product logic |
| R8: Diehl et al., 2016, nine experiments | Taking photographs can increase enjoyment through engagement in some experiences | It can interfere or intensify negative experiences. Receiving a professional photograph is a different intervention |
| R9–R10: sports-tourism studies | Travel behavior varies; unfamiliar practical arrangements can create difficulty | Event-specific observational and qualitative evidence. These studies establish neither demand for FORM trips nor willingness to pay |
| R11: Friederichs et al., 2016, randomized web intervention | An SDT/motivational-interviewing intervention improved one physical-activity outcome | It did not outperform the traditional intervention overall; outcomes were self-reported. A sound theory does not guarantee a superior product |

### Translate mechanisms into restrained service

**Competence:** Help the athlete identify something they can now do, understand or execute better. Use comparable evidence and acknowledge uncertainty. A slower race in different conditions is not automatic regression. A satisfying story is not proof of physiological improvement. The desired result is more informed confidence, not dependence on praise. [R1,R5]

**Autonomy:** Let athletes choose goals and the kind of help they want, while maintaining clear coaching authority over issued prescriptions. Explain decisions when explanation helps. Offer an easy decline of an introduction or gathering. Controlling language and mandatory personal disclosure can make an ostensibly caring service intrusive. These are design implications, not measured FORM effects.

**Practical self-regulation:** Make the next action concrete and identify a realistic contingency with the athlete. “What happens if work runs late?” is more useful than generic motivation. Brice must authorize training alternatives; software can preserve and present them. Removing uncertainty cannot manufacture time, money or energy that someone does not have. [R3]

**Belonging and social capital:** Repeated, low-pressure opportunities can allow familiarity and reciprocal help to develop. A reliable host and an obvious place to stand may matter more than a member directory. Measure a useful encounter or mutually wanted repeat contact, not inferred friendship. Avoid cliques, prestige based on pace, and an expectation that members must contribute labor. [R2,R7]

**Aspiration and self-efficacy:** Show credible possibilities through consenting people's experiences, including ordinary development and revised ambitions. Let someone privately explore an event without appearing on a public commitment list. A fast athlete's story can inspire one person and discourage another; FORM should not assume a common response.

**Recognition and narrative:** A dated, accurate observation can help someone see development they overlooked. A photograph or season page is a hypothesis about memory and meaning, not an evidence-backed mental-health intervention. Never require a triumphant interpretation of disappointment, injury or withdrawal. [R8]

**Adventure and hospitality:** Reduce uncertainty at arrival, identify real logistical constraints, and leave room for personal choice. The hypothesis is that considered arrangements free attention for the experience. More elaborate itineraries can instead raise cost, obligation and cognitive load. [R9,R10]

**Life satisfaction:** FORM can create conditions for meaningful activity, relationships and memorable experiences. This report cannot promise improvements in life satisfaction, mental health or performance from the proposed service. A small pilot should establish concrete usefulness before making broader outcome claims. [R4]

## D. Portfolio: 12 opportunities

Confidence refers to confidence that the opportunity deserves a small test, not certainty of demand or impact. High confidence attaches mainly to correctness and clearly observed friction; almost all new service concepts remain medium or low confidence. Each experiment must be separately authorized before athletes are contacted.

### 1. A dependable filing-to-coach loop

**Need / first beneficiaries:** Know that work was saved correctly and that a question reaches the right person; coached athletes and Brice.

**Real experience / assets:** An athlete files once, sees honest save/delivery status, corrects mistakes, and receives a genuine B. only after Brice reviews the relevant version. Existing Week, receipts, coach pass and versioned marks supply the foundation.

**Why / minimum / app:** Practical reliability, not gamification. Start by observing three consenting test-account journeys, including an offline correction and failed send. App work is likely for verified defects; a manual fallback covers real communication meanwhile.

**Burden / trust:** Roughly 1–2 hours of acceptance work plus engineering; ongoing review time must be measured. A mark cannot resolve an independent coaching concern. Historical records without matching receipt identity must not receive fabricated matches.

**Value test / confidence:** Correct record and recipient every time; no lost work; less repeated entry. Any cross-account exposure or false acknowledgement blocks release. **High priority, grounded in F1,F5–F7; actual incidence unmeasured.**

### 2. A brief explanation at the end of a block

**Need / first beneficiaries:** Understand progress beyond a race time; committed, developing, competitive and remote athletes.

**Real experience / assets:** Brice reviews two relevant pieces of evidence, explains what they do and do not show, and states the next coaching question. Use the existing training record, Dossier and current focus.

**Why / minimum / app:** The hypothesis is improved understanding and confidence. Pilot a ten-minute conversation with four athletes finishing a real block, using an existing channel. No app development needed. [R1,R5]

**Burden / trust:** Estimate 15–20 minutes each including preparation. Keep results private; preserve context and uncertainty rather than selecting only flattering evidence.

**Value test / confidence:** Ask athletes to explain their current focus in their own words before and afterward; check a week later whether the explanation informed a real decision. Continue only if comprehension improves without new reporting. **Medium-high test priority; FORM demand untested.**

### 3. A difficult-week planning appointment

**Need / first beneficiaries:** Resolve a recurring practical obstacle; busy or returning athletes.

**Real experience / assets:** The athlete brings one actual calendar conflict. Brice clarifies the training decision; a helper, if available, handles agreed logistics. The output is a workable week and one contingency, not a new task list.

**Why / minimum / app:** Planning is a plausible mechanism [R3]. Test with three volunteers facing a known disruption, using existing plan tools. No new calendar integration.

**Burden / trust:** Estimate 20 minutes each plus one short follow-up. Do not import full personal calendars or infer availability. An inability to fit everything can justify changing priorities, not pressuring compliance.

**Value test / confidence:** Did the specific obstacle disappear? Count clarification exchanges, athlete coordination time and whether the chosen plan was workable; do not reward extra training volume. **Medium confidence; highly dependent on actual bottlenecks.**

### 4. An agreed plan for the next chapter

**Need / first beneficiaries:** Retain direction after a race, a prolonged interruption or a change in ambition.

**Real experience / assets:** Before the current chapter ends, agree whether the athlete wants a conversation afterward and when. Discuss what matters next, whether they want contact or space, and any next coaching decision. Existing race dates and longitudinal relationships make this feasible.

**Why / minimum / app:** The hypothesis is that continuity prevents people from having to reconstruct the relationship when their goal changes. R6 supports varied motivation, not this service's effectiveness. Pilot three genuine transitions with a 15-minute review and one agreed follow-up. No app build.

**Burden / trust:** Estimate 20–30 minutes per transition. Do not infer injury, low mood or dropout from silence. Clinical care stays with qualified professionals; any participation follows the athlete's existing guidance.

**Value test / confidence:** Athlete can name a wanted next step, including taking time away; follow-up occurred as agreed; no sense of pressure to recommit. **Medium confidence, potentially distinctive.**

### 5. Thursday arrival, deliberately hosted

**Need / first beneficiaries:** Know where to go and feel comfortable joining; new, returning and independent participants.

**Real experience / assets:** An accepted host welcomes people, explains practical arrangements and helps each person find the relevant starting point. A short exit or a different workout is normal when explicitly planned. Thursday's existing recurrence is the asset.

**Why / minimum / app:** Reliable arrival makes repeat participation easier to test [R2,R7]. Pilot two existing Thursdays, with a named host and accurate pre-arrival information. No app development required if the current channel is reliable.

**Burden / trust:** Estimate 15 minutes of preparation and 15 minutes of welcome work per session, preferably shared with an accepted host. No public explanation of an athlete's absence, ability or health; no assumption that proximity means a shared prescription.

**Value test / confidence:** Fewer lost-arrival questions; participants can locate their own work; next attendance is voluntary; host workload stays bounded. **Medium-high fit with existing practice; marginal benefit unproven.**

### 6. One useful introduction

**Need / first beneficiaries:** Find wanted company or practical expertise without entering a social network.

**Real experience / assets:** Brice identifies a specific reason two people might benefit, asks each separately, and makes the introduction only after both agree. Existing relationships provide context; private dossiers do not become matching data.

**Why / minimum / app:** A human introduction reduces initial coordination; repeat shared activity is a plausible benefit [R2,R7]. Pilot up to three introductions around an expressed need, with one optional follow-up. No app build or directory.

**Burden / trust:** Estimate 10–15 minutes per introduction. Share only an approved reason and contact method. A declined offer is closed without explanation to the other person. Membership is not a safety endorsement.

**Value test / confidence:** Did the agreed conversation or activity occur and help? Would both choose another? Count declines and unwanted follow-up. **Medium confidence; density and mutual interest unknown.**

### 7. A practical race-week brief

**Need / first beneficiaries:** Remove logistical uncertainty; confirmed registrants at one event.

**Real experience / assets:** One concise brief gives source-linked event facts, last-checked date, agreed meeting information and the athlete's existing coach-approved instructions in a separate private section. It may include a meal or shakeout only if someone actually hosts it.

**Why / minimum / app:** Specific practical barriers appear in sports-tourism research [R9,R10]. Use the existing race calendar; pilot for one event with confirmed interest. No app development, travel package or booking system.

**Burden / trust:** Estimate 60–90 minutes shared preparation plus ten minutes per athlete; a helper can verify public facts. Recheck material details against organizer sources. Do not expose travel, room details or registrations without consent; group transport and accommodation remain separate decisions.

**Value test / confidence:** Which question or decision did the brief resolve? Measure avoided coordination and errors, not downloads. **Medium-high usefulness for a live need; racecation demand remains low confidence.**

### 8. A temporary home base

**Need / first beneficiaries:** A place to linger, be welcomed and connect after practice; local community participants.

**Real experience / assets:** Reserve a modest café table or permitted space after one existing practice, with clear timing and optional attendance. Brice's hospitality background and the recurring morning provide the assets.

**Why / minimum / app:** Repeated leisure time may support familiarity [R7]; the particular venue is a service hypothesis. Test two dates and a short, predictable window. No lease, app or obligatory spend.

**Burden / trust:** Estimate 30 minutes of organization plus 45 minutes hosting; obtain actual venue terms before committing. Consider accessibility, cost, noise and people who need to leave. Attendance is not a measure of loyalty.

**Value test / confidence:** Count voluntary repeat participation, useful encounters and total hosting cost. If people consistently leave, prefer their existing routine. **Medium-low confidence until local behavior is observed.**

### 9. A practical athlete workshop

**Need / first beneficiaries:** Learn a skill that improves independent judgment; developing or competitive athletes with a shared question.

**Real experience / assets:** A small group practices one skill, such as interpreting a race plan or recording actual interval work accurately. Brice uses authored examples and current coaching expertise. Another coach's athlete retains that coach's prescription.

**Why / minimum / app:** The hypothesis is that guided practice develops competence. Start with a 30-minute session for four to six interested people; offer a remote equivalent only if wanted. No course platform. [R1,R5]

**Burden / trust:** Estimate 60–90 minutes total preparation and delivery. Use fictional or explicitly authorized examples; avoid public diagnosis of technique or ability.

**Value test / confidence:** Can the athlete perform the skill later without help? Stop if it adds homework without solving a real question. **Medium confidence; better as requested teaching than a content calendar.**

### 10. A warm handoff to relevant expertise

**Need / first beneficiaries:** Obtain help outside FORM's remit without repeated searching; athletes who express a concrete need.

**Real experience / assets:** With permission, Brice helps formulate the question and introduces an appropriate qualified professional. FORM's relational role is the asset; the existence of an established referral network is not assumed.

**Why / minimum / app:** This is a practical continuity hypothesis, not a claim of clinical benefit. Start with one requested referral; verify professional scope, availability, cost and any relationship before recommending. No app build.

**Burden / trust:** Estimate 30–60 minutes to establish each partner relationship and 10–15 minutes per handoff. The athlete chooses what information to share. No automatic medical-record transfer or undisclosed referral incentive.

**Value test / confidence:** Was the appropriate appointment or conversation obtained, was the question answered, and did the handoff reduce repetition? **Medium-low until an actual need and suitable partner exist.**

### 11. A private season record

**Need / first beneficiaries:** Retain something meaningful about development beyond public results; remote, quiet and milestone athletes.

**Real experience / assets:** A single page with one approved photograph, two verified moments and the athlete's own reflection. The athlete may keep it private, revise the interpretation or decline it. FORM has photography and a training record.

**Why / minimum / app:** Meaningful recognition is the hypothesis; R8 does not prove receiving such an artifact changes motivation. Make three examples only after discussing whether the recipients want them. No app development or public feed.

**Burden / trust:** Estimate 30–45 minutes per page. Facts, image rights and publication permission are separate checks. Include an ordinary or difficult season without forcing a redemption story.

**Value test / confidence:** Does the athlete retain, revisit or voluntarily request another? A social repost is neither required nor the primary measure. **Low-to-medium confidence; strong cultural fit, uncertain incremental value.**

### 12. An athletic opportunity appointment

**Need / first beneficiaries:** Make one personally meaningful possibility feasible; people whose ambition extends beyond their next prescribed race.

**Real experience / assets:** Ask what athletic experience they would like to have but have not known how to arrange. It might be a first independently navigated event, learning from a specialist, hosting a morning, or a local exploration compatible with their plan. Brice helps resolve one barrier or make one introduction.

**Why / minimum / app:** This tests whether FORM can expand agency and access beyond training. It is intentionally an H, not a proven mechanism. Pilot three 25-minute appointments with different current circumstances and a single follow-through action each. No app build.

**Burden / trust:** Cap effort at 45 minutes per person, including follow-through. No promise of privileged access, funding or travel. Keep ambitions private and do not convert an idea into a public commitment.

**Value test / confidence:** Within an agreed period, did one concrete step occur that was previously blocked, and was FORM instrumental? If conversations produce only pleasant possibilities, stop. **Low confidence, high learning value; the strongest unconventional experiment.**

## E. Three exceptional moments

These are hypothetical service scenes, not accounts of actual athletes.

**An unhurried Thursday arrival.** A returning athlete arrives knowing exactly where to meet. The host greets them normally, without announcing a comeback. Their own agreed session remains separate from the group's. Someone points out where they can finish and leave on time. There is an invitation to coffee, with no explanation needed if they decline. The thoughtfulness is that nobody makes their return harder than it already is.

**The race-week question that is already answered.** An athlete checks the brief and finds the organizer's actual pickup window and an agreed solution to a practical scheduling conflict. They do not have to reconstruct details from several chats. Their private instructions remain private, and a shared dinner is clearly optional. Afterward, Brice follows up at the time they agreed, whether the race went well or poorly.

**Progress made visible without exaggeration.** A remote athlete finishes a block disappointed by a headline result. Brice shows two genuinely comparable observations, explains the limits, and identifies what has developed and what remains uncertain. They agree on the next question. The athlete receives a short record of that conversation in their usual channel. They feel understood because the explanation is precise, not because it is relentlessly positive.

## G. A small, respectful validation program

### Four-week discovery and pilot sequence

**Week 1: establish the baseline and recruit by circumstance.** Invite roughly 10–12 adults from the approximately 35 active records, without treating membership as consent to research. Seek overlap across local/remote, coached/independent, regular/occasional participation, app/non-app use and people near a transition. Include people who use FORM lightly and at least two who are less enthusiastic or have recently declined something, if they consent. These are sampling goals, not known roster characteristics. If unavailable, state the gap.

Use 20–25 minute interviews. An independent interviewer, if available, can reduce pressure to please Brice; do not pretend anonymity in a small roster. Explain that criticism or declining research will not affect service. Keep pseudonymous notes and explicit recording consent; collect only necessary context. Offer a proposed 30-day deletion date for raw recordings, subject to the agreed research arrangement.

**Week 2: observe real work.** Observe three consenting filing or question-resolution journeys, two existing hosted sessions, and the preparation for one actual upcoming race if one is available. Ask permission before looking at messages. Measure steps, repeated information, avoidable exchanges, unresolved questions and time spent by athlete, Brice and any host. Do not scrape private chats or infer attendance from GPS.

**Weeks 3–4: run only two service experiments.** Select from observed needs. The default candidates are four block explanations and one hosted-arrival or race-brief pilot. Substitute the opportunity appointment if interviews reveal unmet ambitions and little routine friction. Do not run all 12 ideas. Keep existing essential service available; no safety or communication support is withheld to create a comparison group.

### Questions that do not sell the concept

1. Walk me through the last week when training was harder to fit into your life than expected. What happened next?
2. Show me the last time you were unsure what to do, whether something had saved, or whom to ask. How did you resolve it?
3. What have you asked Brice more than once? What information have you had to provide repeatedly?
4. Tell me about the last race or gathering you considered and decided against. What made the decision?
5. When has being around other athletes helped? When has it made things harder or simply been unwanted?
6. What do you currently believe is improving? What evidence makes you think that?
7. What changed after your last race or interruption? What help, if any, did you seek?
8. What do you value enough to arrange yourself already? What would you prefer FORM leave alone?
9. What would you miss if FORM disappeared? What would you easily replace?
10. Is there something you have wanted to do that has remained difficult to arrange? What have you tried?

Only after discussing actual behavior should the interviewer show a specific prototype or describe a service. Ask what it would replace, what would make it annoying, and what the person would realistically do next. “Would you use this?” is weak evidence.

### Practical decision rules

| Test | Continue signal | Stop or revise signal |
|---|---|---|
| Filing/delivery | All observed records correct; failed paths preserve work and honest status | Any lost work, incorrect identity or misleading delivery claim blocks expansion |
| Four block explanations | At least three can explain the focus and next gate later; at least two cite a useful subsequent decision | Polite praise without understanding; explanation creates more reporting or confusion |
| Hosted arrival | Fewer practical questions than baseline, no prescription confusion, workable host effort | Extra coordination exceeds benefit; newcomers remain confused; social pressure is reported |
| Race brief | Confirmed participants identify specific resolved questions; public facts are current | Nobody has a concrete use, duplication of organizer material adds burden, or errors appear |
| Introduction | Both independently report that the contact was wanted and useful | Either reports pressure or unwanted contact; do not repeat that pairing |
| Opportunity appointment | At least two of three take a previously blocked concrete step within the agreed follow-up window | Conversation remains aspirational or requires persistent chasing |

These are proposed management thresholds for very small pilots, not tests of statistical significance. Report individual outcomes and denominators, including declines and incomplete follow-up. Missing data is unknown, not failure or success. Repeated voluntary use is stronger than compliments, but neither proves causal impact.

Keep a short record: problem, current workaround, agreed intervention, actual outcome, minutes spent by each party, costs, negative effects and next decision. A task can be worth paying staff to do even if total minutes do not fall; make that tradeoff explicit. Do not label a shift of work from athlete to Brice as net time saved.

**Intrusiveness signals:** declining twice, apologizing for not attending, hiding setbacks to preserve recognition, feeling obliged to reply, surprise about remembered personal information, or asking why someone knows their plans. Pause the relevant service, ask what boundary they want, and preserve unrelated access. One serious privacy or trust incident outweighs several positive ratings.

## H. Practical operating model

### Separate judgment, logistics and records

| Owner | Appropriate work | Boundary |
|---|---|---|
| Brice | Prescriptions, interpretation, sensitive conversations, personal observations, service promises and final judgment on a proposed introduction | Do not delegate his signature or imply he personally reviewed work he has not seen |
| Accepted host or coordinator, if staffed | Arrival, venue arrangements, public race facts, agreed logistics, confirmations and operational follow-through | Access only to information needed for the role; cannot alter training or browse private dossiers |
| Qualified partners | Services within their professional scope and agreed availability | Athlete consent governs handoffs; no assumed partnership or hidden incentives |
| Software | Versioned records, explicit preferences, authorized access, delivery state, factual dates, draft preservation and administrative reminders to the responsible operator | No inferred friendship, registration, attendance, consent or coach attention |
| AI | Prepare a source-linked evidence packet, summarize authorized records for review, identify missing facts and draft operational material | No automatic B., outbound impersonation, medical inference, new prescription, or promotion of a draft to accepted truth |

The coordinator and partner roles are proposed responsibilities, not claims that people currently occupy them. Until someone accepts a role, its work remains Brice's or the service is not offered.

### Capacity before promises

For an initial pilot, cap additional service work at **three hours per week**, excluding existing coaching and one-time engineering work. A possible allocation is 30 minutes reviewing approaching transitions, 60 minutes for selected explanations, 45 minutes for one operational pilot, and 45 minutes of contingency and follow-through. Log actual time for four weeks and revise.

A universal ten-minute weekly note to 35 people would require 350 minutes, or 5 hours 50 minutes, before preparation and replies. That is why selective, relevant contact is preferable to a ceremonial cadence. Similarly, acknowledgement effort depends on actual filings: an illustrative 175 filings at 30 seconds each is 87.5 minutes before interpretation. These are capacity illustrations, not observed usage.

Define the promise for each service relationship: how to contact FORM, what the person can count on, a realistic response window, who covers an absence, and what is outside scope. Do not invent a universal response-time promise before measuring the workload. Event-only participants can receive accurate logistics without being enrolled in a coaching service.

Maintain a small internal list of real responsibilities: owner, due occasion, source, status and next action. A delayed reply should not disappear because the coach left a B. Elsewhere, an empty queue can be correct. Do not turn people into retention-risk scores.

At growth, add capacity where work actually accumulates. A coordinator can absorb repeated logistics; a qualified coach can own defined coaching relationships; a trained host can carry a morning. If 70 members would simply double Brice's personal obligations, cap intake or narrow the offer before expanding. No research establishes 35, 70 or any other number as the optimal intimacy threshold.

Track service hours, direct costs, work transferred between people, unresolved responsibilities and voluntary repeat use. Pricing requires actual costs and willingness-to-pay evidence; the $500 feeling is not evidence for a $500 price. Member labor, photos and public testimonials should never become the hidden subsidy for care.

## I. Five attractive traps

| Reject or defer | Why it is tempting and what it costs | Better alternative |
|---|---|---|
| A social graph, feed or automatic athlete matching | Visible activity can look like community; it introduces permissions, moderation, sparse-network and comparison problems | A few double-consent introductions and dependable existing gatherings |
| Universal personal messages and automated recognition | It looks scalable and attentive; it consumes time or manufactures authorship | Selective human observations, source-grounded preparation and honest operational notices |
| A clubhouse or packaged racecation now | It embodies the culture; it adds fixed cost, hospitality/travel obligations and assumes demand | A temporary table and one practical brief for confirmed racers |
| More analytics, readiness scores or public comparisons | They look sophisticated; they can create false precision, explanation burden and competitive pressure | A short evidence-based block explanation and accurate current focus |
| An encyclopedic person file or all-purpose concierge | It promises deep personalization; it collects sensitive details, becomes stale and expands the service beyond capacity | Minimum necessary preferences and context, expiry/review where useful, and an agreed athletic remit |

FORM should never become a surveillance-based community, a system that requires public performance to receive care, a substitute for clinical judgment, or a service that treats every quiet period as a retention emergency. Nor should it make existing coaching less useful to drive app adoption.

## J. Final recommendation

1. **Strongest strategic direction:** A personal athletic service that preserves continuity. Its promise is that athletes can trust the next step, understand their development and obtain selected help when circumstances change. Hosted practice and bounded development projects support that promise.
2. **Three highest-value actions for existing athletes:** First, prove the filing, correction and communication loop and remove confirmed repeated effort. Second, pilot brief explanations at real block or season transitions. Third, make existing gatherings and confirmed race commitments operationally dependable through accepted hosts, honest status and concise practical information.
3. **One unconventional experiment:** Three athletic opportunity appointments, each ending in one bounded action that removes a real barrier. Test whether FORM can make a personally meaningful experience possible beyond the next training prescription.
4. **Largest unresolved question:** Is the scarce thing in this roster better interpretation, dependable coordination, wanted companionship, or access to experiences? The founder's strengths make all four plausible; the current evidence cannot rank athlete demand confidently.
5. **Most important evidence before committing resources:** Recent, specific episodes in which athletes struggled or abandoned something; observation of their actual workaround; and a manual intervention that demonstrably resolves the problem at a sustainable cost. Repeated voluntary use, including among quiet and non-app participants, matters more than enthusiasm for the vision.

The opportunity is substantial precisely because FORM already knows people over time. The next investment should show where that knowledge changes what becomes possible in their lives.
