# FORM
## The Athletic Life
### Independent Strategy, Human Experience & Product Opportunity Research Brief

**Prepared:** October 28, 2026  
**Founder and coach:** Brice Ikouebe  
**Organization:** Speed & Form / FORM  
**Stage:** Existing athletic roster, operational coaching system, developing iOS application and real-world collective  
**Research status:** Exploratory. This brief is not authorization to build, publish, contact athletes, change prescriptions or modify production systems.

---

## 1. The central assignment

We want to discover what FORM could become if we started with one question:

**How can we meaningfully improve the lives of the athletes and people already connected to FORM?**

Not merely how to improve their running. Not merely how to make a better training app.

How can FORM help people perform better, live more interesting lives, develop meaningful relationships, pursue ambitions, experience adventure, and spend less time managing the logistics of doing the things that matter to them?

We already have athletes, relationships, coaching knowledge, training records, races, recurring practice, photographs, history, and a recognizable culture.

The opportunity may lie in making better use of what already exists.

We want to examine opportunities across:

- Athletic performance, physical capability and long-term development.
- Psychology, motivation, identity, habits and human flourishing.
- Friendships, belonging, trust and shared experiences.
- Racing, travel, exploration and adventure.
- Organization, coordination, planning and reduced mental overhead.
- Personal coaching, interpretation and thoughtful human attention.
- Photography, storytelling, personal history and recognition.
- Hospitality, physical gathering places, and experiences worth remembering.
- Any other opportunity that the evidence suggests, even if it is outside conventional fitness software.

**Do not assume that the answer is more features.**

A better service, introduction, ritual, physical experience, partnership, coach workflow, or entirely different product direction may be more valuable than another app screen.

We want independent thinking, informed by research and actual people.

## 2. What FORM is today

FORM is the athlete-facing system and athletic collective connected to Speed & Form, a Miami-based endurance coaching practice led by Brice.

It is not intended to be another Strava, TrainingPeaks, social network, or generic running club.

It combines a serious approach to individualized athletic development with a real community of people who train, race, and occasionally share experiences.

### The founder's particular strengths

Brice's approach combines several roles:

**Coach.** He authors structured training, observes athletes, interprets evidence and develops people over time. He values movement quality, mechanics, speed, durability, fitness, and authentic performance improvements.

**Connector.** He naturally sees relationships between people and enjoys bringing people together. He is interested in what makes an individual distinctive, not merely their sporting results.

**Host.** He previously spent years operating a café and has a strong hospitality instinct. Arrival, atmosphere, remembering details, a sense of welcome, and an experience that feels thoughtfully arranged are familiar territory.

**Observer and storyteller.** Photography, filmmaking, portraits, athletic records and the narrative of someone's development are integral to the FORM world.

**Human-development practitioner.** Athletic training is a vehicle for broader personal growth. Discipline, composure, confidence, competence, relationships, purpose and self-respect matter beyond the finish line.

Brice often describes the desired service quality as having a **$500-per-month feeling**, even when a particular experience is free. This is a standard of care and execution, not a pricing proposal.

His vision favors small, meaningful, in-person relationships over an enormous anonymous network.

The app is not the entire product. It is the surface of a broader service.

## 3. The existing athlete population

This assignment must begin with the actual roster, not an invented mass-market persona.

### Verified aggregate snapshot

A read-only production database inspection on October 28, 2026 found:

| Measure | Recorded |
|---|---:|
| Athlete records | 36 |
| Active athlete records | 35 |
| Athletes with active coach-account membership links | 34 |
| Athletes with at least one training block | 25 |
| Active training blocks | 24 |
| Athletes associated with race entries | 17 |
| Race events recorded | 13 |
| Athlete race entries | 26 |
| Entries explicitly marked registered | 7 |
| Entries marked target or alternative | 19 |

These are database counts, not claims about app engagement, attendance, paying customers, actual training compliance, or confirmed race travel.

A coach-account membership link is not proof that an athlete is purchasing individual coaching. Similarly, a target race is not a confirmed registration.

Investigate actual use before inferring demand.

### The types of people represented

From coaching discussions, the roster and existing product direction, FORM spans multiple overlapping populations:

**Structured coached runners.** Athletes pursuing half marathons, marathons, speed development and long-term performance improvements.

**Fast and competitive athletes.** Experienced runners, former competitive athletes and people with demanding performance ambitions.

**Developing or returning athletes.** People rebuilding consistency, preparing for a challenge or trying to sustain an athletic life around work and other commitments.

**Hybrid and HYROX participants.** People combining running, strength, conditioning and competition.

**Independent athletes.** People who may already have their own coach, training plan, watch or training platform and do not need FORM to replace it.

**Community- and event-oriented people.** People attracted by a trustworthy environment, familiar faces, good training mornings and participation rather than a comprehensive coached plan.

**Local and remote participants.** Miami is the social anchor, but training and racing may occur elsewhere. Some people can regularly attend group practice; others cannot.

These are working segments, not measured psychological profiles. Individual athletes can belong to several.

### Key question

What does each kind of person need that they are not currently receiving, and how do those needs change during a season, after a race, during a setback, or when their relationship with training changes?

Do not assume every athlete wants more connection, more competition, more data or more coaching.

## 4. What already makes FORM distinctive

FORM already has several assets that could be difficult for a new app to reproduce.

### A real practice

Thursday track work is a recurring anchor. Athletes may share a session structure but have individually prescribed paces, distances, adjustments and goals.

Monday iRUN and optional Saturday gatherings also exist in the broader practice. Not every Saturday is a formally hosted FORM run.

**A shared place or session does not mean everyone has the same prescription.**

### An actual race network

The Race Calendar can show the people competing at events, across full and half marathons, HYROX and other races.

When viewed visually, faces, dates, race names and other athletes' ambitions make upcoming events feel real.

This can create curiosity. A person may discover that several familiar athletes are going to an event and begin to consider participating themselves.

That possibility is particularly interesting, but it remains a hypothesis requiring user research.

### A substantial private coaching record

FORM has training plans, prescribed sessions, recorded work, pace information, races, progress and individualized coaching context.

This information can support better service and decisions.

Private health, recovery, performance and personal details must not automatically become community information.

### A recognizable group of people

FORM athletes are not interchangeable anonymous users.

They have faces, histories, interests, ambitions and relationships with Brice and sometimes with one another.

The founder's trust and curation are an asset. However, membership is not a personal safety certification or automatic permission to share information or introduce people.

### A visual and cultural world

Photography, race portraits, editorial design and an athlete's own story already matter to the experience.

FORM aims for understated excellence: beautiful, composed, effortless, personal, serious about training, and free of unnecessary gamification.

The opportunity may arise from bringing these existing strengths together rather than inventing unrelated product categories.

## 5. Existing product capabilities and boundaries

The application already contains, or is developing, several connected experiences:

- **Week:** the athlete's issued training, the current day's work, and filing sessions.
- **Training record and receipt:** what the athlete actually performed, with evidence and corrections.
- **Dossier:** the athlete's season, focus, development and history.
- **Race Calendar:** races, dates, participants and athlete-specific race information.
- **Collective / hosted runs:** real training gatherings, joining and operational details.
- **Coach surfaces:** roster, plan management, filings, exceptions, personal context and review.
- **Private coach messages:** direct conversation, with notification delivery still requiring real-device validation.
- **Concierge acknowledgements:** a personal `B.` mark showing that Brice witnessed an actual filing.
- **Notification and communication infrastructure:** partly present, but not all delivery and reminder concepts are fully accepted or released.

Several proposed experiences are not yet built or device-verified. Do not confuse a design document, migration, feature flag or code branch with a working athlete experience.

The current product direction rejects excessive notifications, generic motivational filler, public performance comparisons, meaningless scores and activity feeds designed to drive engagement for its own sake.

An athlete's training remains individualized and coach-authoritative.

The app should reduce friction instead of demanding more reporting, decisions or administrative work.

## 6. The human questions behind the opportunity

We are especially interested in how FORM could help people achieve more than they would on their own.

Consider several dimensions without assuming any is universally important.

### Competence and progress

What makes an athlete genuinely feel that they are improving?

Is it faster performance, visible development, learning how their body works, confidently handling difficult sessions, maintaining consistency, or being able to do something previously impossible?

What information helps someone learn, rather than simply giving them another number?

What can a coach notice that a generic algorithm cannot?

### Autonomy and reduced decision fatigue

How much benefit comes from not having to organize everything yourself?

Training is easier to act on when the next step is clear, accurately prescribed and connected to the person's actual life.

Could FORM remove the need to decide where to train, who is available, how a run fits the week, which race to consider, and what preparation is necessary?

Where does helpful orchestration become controlling or overbearing?

### Relatedness and belonging

How do familiar faces, shared practice, recognition and a sense of membership influence motivation?

Does knowing someone else is preparing for the same race change the experience of training?

Can a group create useful companionship without social pressure, performance comparison or compulsory participation?

Can people feel part of FORM even when they live elsewhere?

### Identity and aspiration

How does someone come to think of themselves as a runner, competitor, capable person or member of a disciplined athletic group?

Can seeing the ambitions and progress of others help someone imagine a new possibility for themselves?

Where is the boundary between constructive aspiration and unhealthy social comparison?

### Adventure and novelty

Could races and shared training serve as catalysts for travel, exploration, new places and experiences outside ordinary routines?

What makes someone decide to take a trip, enter a race, discover a new place or join others for a weekend?

What would reduce the anxiety or complexity involved?

### Human recognition

What makes somebody feel remembered?

Is it a thoughtful question, a specific observation, an introduction, a photo, the acknowledgment of a milestone, or somebody noticing progress that otherwise went unseen?

When is automation useful, and when does it reduce the value of the personal gesture?

### Reciprocity and contribution

How might athletes help one another without FORM becoming an attention economy?

Could expertise, encouragement, introductions, familiarity and shared experiences flow between people naturally?

How can the culture preserve generosity without imposing a requirement to socialize?

## 7. Research areas and evidence to investigate

Look beyond sports-app design.

Investigate relevant findings from:

- Self-determination theory: autonomy, competence, relatedness and autonomous motivation.
- Social identity, belonging, social support and group exercise.
- Exercise adherence, planning, routines and the intention–behavior gap.
- Behavioral design, friction reduction and appropriately timed prompts.
- Self-efficacy, mastery, feedback and personal achievement.
- Aspirational identity and social comparison.
- Friendship formation, repeated exposure and shared activities.
- Social capital, trust, reciprocity and community formation.
- Goal setting, personal development and meaningful life experiences.
- Sports tourism, travel motivation and experiential hospitality.
- Service design, anticipatory assistance and high-end concierge operations.
- Coaching relationships, athlete autonomy and confidence.
- Rituals, memory, photography and personal narrative.

**Research starting points**

1. Xu et al., 2025, systematic review of self-determination theory and physical activity: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40256835/
2. Franken et al., 2022, *Running Together: How Sports Partners Keep You Running*: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35368415/
3. Yang et al., 2025, exercise intention–behavior gap and planning: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/40470022/
4. Systematic review of psychological and behavioral correlates of recreational running: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8139406/
5. Systematic review of group fitness adherence and psychosocial predictors: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42352717/
6. 2026 review of amateur marathon participation motivations: https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40359-026-05550-x

These studies are starting points, not evidence that any proposed FORM feature will work.

Distinguish causal evidence from associations, and strong research from weak or context-specific findings. Do not use neuroscience, habit formation or psychology as decorative explanations for a product idea.

## 8. Illustrative opportunities, not instructions

We already have some intuitive possibilities.

For example, seeing familiar athletes registered for a race might create interest in joining them. A small group traveling to the same event might share logistics, a meal, an easy shakeout run, or some time exploring the city.

One could imagine a concept loosely described as a **racecation**: a race becomes an occasion for an athletic and social adventure.

Similarly, someone who usually trains alone might enjoy discovering that another FORM athlete sometimes runs in the same general area.

Or an athlete who already receives precise coaching might benefit more from removing logistical uncertainty than from receiving additional training analytics.

These ideas are prompts to investigate, not preferred recommendations.

**Do not organize the entire strategy around racecations, athlete matching or social networking simply because they have been mentioned.**

Investigate whether a more compelling opportunity exists.

It may involve better training interpretation, an improved coaching relationship, a physical clubhouse, athlete storytelling, curated experiences, practical life organization, mentorship, knowledge sharing, access to opportunities, or something nobody has yet considered.

Search for overlooked needs, including needs that athletes may not spontaneously describe as app features.

## 9. A service, not necessarily a software feature

For every promising opportunity, ask what the best solution actually is.

Could it be delivered by:

- The existing app, using data already available?
- A small improvement to coaching operations?
- A human introduction or thoughtful message?
- A recurring in-person practice or hosted experience?
- A partnership or a trusted specialist?
- A curated race, retreat or travel experience?
- A better piece of athlete information at the right moment?
- A physical artifact, photograph, annual record or personal milestone?
- Something that should deliberately remain outside the app?

Do not assume more technology makes the service more premium.

Also ask whether the founder's time is being spent where human judgment matters most, and whether software can remove repetitive work without manufacturing intimacy.

A concept that saves five minutes but requires ten minutes of explanation is unlikely to meet FORM's standard.

## 10. The people who must benefit

Evaluate ideas through several real user circumstances.

**The committed coached athlete:** They already know Brice and trust their training. What would create additional value beyond a weekly prescription?

**The competitive athlete:** They are ambitious, perhaps experienced and already capable of managing training. What can FORM genuinely add to performance, opportunity or experience?

**The developing athlete:** They want to become more capable but may be less confident or experienced. How can FORM support growth without making comparison intimidating?

**The independent athlete:** They may have another coach and use another app. How can FORM make their athletic life richer without demanding control of their training?

**The social athlete:** They value people, a place to belong and events. Can they receive meaningful value without filing every workout or buying coaching?

**The remote athlete:** They cannot regularly attend Miami gatherings. How can their membership still feel real, useful and personal?

**The busy athlete:** They enjoy training but have limited time and attention. What can be organized, anticipated or simplified on their behalf?

**Brice, the coach:** He needs to retain an accurate mental model of everyone, notice relevant changes, preserve relationships, remember important events and coach effectively without becoming buried in administration.

Do not assume these are mutually exclusive personas.

Challenge the segmentation. Propose a more useful way to understand the roster if the evidence supports it.

## 11. Important ethical and product boundaries

The following are established constraints.

**Private data is not community data.** Training history, medical context, injury notes, personal records, coach observations and location information remain protected unless a specific, authorized sharing action exists.

**Connection is voluntary.** Do not automatically expose somebody's training schedule, home location, private goals, attendance or travel plans because they are in the same network.

**The coach remains the coach.** Shared activities cannot silently replace individual prescriptions. AI may support interpretation and administration but cannot autonomously rewrite the prescribed training.

**Trust is cultural, not an absolute safety guarantee.** Membership may create familiarity, but physical meetings, travel, introductions and accommodation arrangements still require appropriate consent, boundaries and practical safeguards.

**No manufactured engagement.** Avoid streak pressure, attendance guilt, gamified care, social ranking, engagement-bait notifications or artificial motivational language.

**The personal relationship must remain authentic.** Automated messages must never masquerade as Brice's personal observations or attention.

**The system must remain useful for athletes who do not install the app.** FORM's human network and service should not disappear when someone uses other tools.

**The design standard is premium restraint.** Selective editorial typography, beautiful photography, strong readability, clear interaction and no unnecessary explanation. Personal text and reading content use clean sans-serif typography.

**Reality before marketing.** Do not claim actual participation, attendance, registration, performance improvement, friendship or an experience merely because a user considered it.

## 12. What we want you to discover

Approach this as an independent strategic investigation.

We are not asking for a generic fitness-app feature wishlist, a luxury-brand moodboard, or confirmation that our current ideas are good.

We want an informed answer to:

**What are the highest-leverage ways FORM could improve the athletic lives and broader human experiences of this particular group of people, starting from what already exists?**

Explore the possibility that our largest opportunities are not immediately obvious.

Challenge our assumptions. Identify needs we may be overlooking. Separate powerful opportunities from attractive but low-value ideas.

Consider whether FORM could become something meaningfully distinctive at the intersection of athletic development, shared ambition, human connection, adventure, hospitality and trusted personal service.

What would be difficult for Strava, Garmin, Nike Run Club or another generic platform to reproduce because FORM has real relationships, real coaching judgment and a real local community?

Equally important: what should FORM deliberately never become?

## 13. Required independent-agent deliverables

Please return a structured, evidence-grounded report containing the following.

### A. Your independent thesis

In plain language, describe what FORM could uniquely become and why.

Present at least three meaningfully different strategic futures. Do not make all three variations of a social running app.

Explain which future you favor, what assumptions it requires, and what could make it wrong.

### B. A needs and opportunity map

Identify the most important unmet or poorly served needs of the current athlete population.

For each, distinguish:

- Known evidence from FORM's actual operations.
- Published external research.
- Reasonable inference.
- An untested hypothesis.

Explain which people are affected and how valuable solving the problem might be.

### C. The psychology of the FORM experience

Identify the psychological and social mechanisms that could legitimately improve performance, consistency, self-belief, autonomy, belonging and life satisfaction.

Explain the likely mechanisms, limitations and possible harmful effects.

Do not assume more social exposure, notifications or performance comparison is universally better.

### D. Ten to fifteen opportunities

Provide a portfolio spanning meaningful product improvements, human services, physical experiences, partnerships and unconventional possibilities.

For each opportunity, include:

- The actual human need.
- Who benefits first.
- What the experience looks like in real life.
- What existing FORM assets make it possible.
- Why it may work psychologically or practically.
- The minimum credible version.
- Whether it needs app development.
- Operational burden on Brice.
- Privacy, safety or trust considerations.
- How we would test genuine value.
- Confidence and supporting evidence.

Seek opportunities that create distinct value rather than ten versions of a notification, social feed or race calendar.

### E. Three exceptional moments

Describe three experiences an athlete could have through FORM that would make them privately think:

*Somebody really thought this through.*

At least one should be primarily physical or human, rather than digital.

Avoid exaggerated luxury rituals. Make the quality come from relevance, precision, effortlessness or meaningful care.

### F. What to improve immediately

Identify the most valuable improvements to the current experience for the existing roster.

Focus on friction reduction, coaching usefulness, trustworthy data, race visibility, actual training coordination, personal relationships and service continuity.

Explain which changes should be made first and which should wait.

### G. How to validate this with athletes

Propose a small, respectful research program appropriate for roughly 35 active roster records.

Include a plan for qualitative athlete interviews, real-behavior observation and low-cost experiments.

Suggest what to ask without leading people toward our existing ideas.

Use evidence of actual decisions, attendance, helpful introductions, races selected, time saved or improved understanding rather than app opens and vanity engagement metrics.

Describe how to detect when an idea is not wanted, becomes intrusive, or creates administrative work without sufficient benefit.

### H. A practical operating model

Explain what Brice should personally own, what staff or partners could handle, and what software or AI could perform safely.

Consider how the service could remain excellent if the roster grew, without destroying the intimacy that creates its value.

Separate a service that requires genuine human judgment from an administrative process that could be automated.

### I. What not to build

Identify five attractive traps, misleading assumptions or features you would reject or defer.

Explain the opportunity cost and the better alternative.

### J. Final recommendation

End with:

1. The strongest strategic direction.
2. The three highest-value actions for existing athletes.
3. One compelling but unconventional experiment.
4. The largest unresolved question.
5. The most important evidence to gather before committing resources.

Do not simply recommend expanding FORM into a social platform. Earn that conclusion if the evidence supports it.

## 15. The quality bar for the answer

Think as a multidisciplinary group of experienced practitioners: high-end service designer, elite performance coach, sport psychologist, behavioral scientist, anthropologist, community builder, hospitality operator, product strategist and systems architect.

But do not force every discipline into the answer. Follow the opportunity.

The research should be:

**Independent:** willing to disagree with our current direction.

**Specific:** grounded in this roster, these relationships and the actual FORM environment.

**Human:** about real experiences rather than feature counts.

**Rigorous:** clear about evidence, uncertainty and causality.

**Imaginative:** willing to discover opportunities outside familiar categories.

**Operational:** honest about work, staffing, consent and implementation.

**Tasteful:** consistent with the understated, exceptionally thoughtful service FORM intends to provide.

The ambition is not to have the most features or the largest community.

It is to become extraordinarily valuable to the people who choose to be part of FORM.

**Final question to keep in mind:**

*What could FORM make possible in an athlete's life that probably would not have happened, or would have been significantly harder, without FORM?*
